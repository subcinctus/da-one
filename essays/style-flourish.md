# Style guide: giving Karan's essays flourish (11 October 2026)

Karan's instruction: give his essays "flourish like that text". "That text" is another applicant's statement (`flourish/sample_flourish.md`). Use it only as a model of shape, voice and level of flourish. Never copy its words, facts or ideas.

This guide replaces the "less flourish" part of the 8 October brief (`style_brief.md`). Everything in that brief about truth, tone and format still applies (see section 2). Karan's own facts are in `corrected_facts.md`, `flourish/photo_story_verbatim.md`, `flourish/facts_addendum.md` and `flourish/facts_check.md`. How much of the photography story to use is in `flourish/usage_rule.md`. Where this guide and those files disagree on a fact, the fact files win.

Main test for every essay: **the flourish comes from the arc, the framing idea, the named credits and the sentence rhythm. It never comes from adding facts, adjectives or drama.**

---

## 1. What the sample does

### 1.1 Narrative arc
The sample follows one line, in this order:
1. **Identity tension** (para 1). Who the writer is, and the problem that comes with it.
2. **Naming the concept** (end of para 1). A college class gives the writer a word for what they had been doing all along.
3. **Method** (paras 2 and 3). The concept becomes a way of working: research, classes, minors.
4. **Passion origin** (para 4). A small, homemade beginning (YouTube music videos) and what it taught.
5. **Proof** (para 5). A dense paragraph of jobs, employers and credits, each with a role.
6. **Calling** (para 6). What all of this showed them they want to do.
7. **Mission** (para 7). The current job, then a close that brings back the concept from para 1, now as something to give others.

Each paragraph does one job and hands on to the next. Nothing is thrown in for colour alone.

### 1.2 Identity framing
The opening places the writer inside a specific identity and names both what it gives and what it costs. The identity then explains the behaviour (watching films to learn a culture), and the behaviour becomes the career. The identity is never a sob story. It is the reason the writer pays attention the way they do.

### 1.3 The tying concept: cultural capital
The sample names one concept, late in para 1, as a realisation ("what I had been doing was..."). It returns in para 3 ("this form of cultural capital") and again in the final sentence, where the writer turns from gaining it to giving it to others. That return is what makes the essay feel designed, not listed. **Karan needs one concept used the same way: named early, used once in the middle, brought back at the close.** His is the frame (section 3).

### 1.4 Specific names and credits
The sample names nearly everything: UCLA, Sociology of Education, Lifetime's Dance Moms, Beau Flynn with his credit in brackets, Studio 22, SAGE Publishing. It gives roles and progressions ("first as an office assistant... and then eventually a video production assistant"). Named, checkable detail is what lets an essay with this much feeling still read as credible.

### 1.5 Sentence rhythm
- Long, flowing sentences (often 25 to 40 words) built on subordinate clauses, with a short plain sentence now and then.
- Concessive openings: "While I am lucky to..., I have struggled...".
- Delayed realisation: "It was not until [a specific moment] that I realized...".
- Condition and consequence: "I knew early on that if I wanted to..., I had to...".
- Learning from evidence: "It only took a few videos for me to notice that...", followed by a concrete list (audio, framing, editing, lighting).
- Lists of three or four nouns with the serial comma.
- Ladders of roles and verbs: "produced, directed, filmed, and edited".
- A conditional close: "if..., that would be more than enough to...".

### 1.6 Passion to proof to mission
Feeling comes first, in one paragraph, tied to a real beginning. Proof follows straight away, and it is the longest and densest stretch. The mission comes last and is built on the proof. The writer never asks the reader to take the passion on trust.

### 1.7 Aspirational close
The last paragraph starts from a real present (a first job, named) and widens to a hope larger than the writer. It is aspirational but stays conditional ("if... by watching one video").

### 1.8 What not to take from the sample
- "A blessing and a curse", "true calling", "change the world", "effect change on society as a whole". These are clichés or overclaims. Karan's brief rules them out.
- Its facts, its concept (cultural capital) and its identity. Karan has his own.

## 2. What to keep from the 8 October brief

These still hold. Breaking one is a defect.

1. **No invented facts.** Every event, role, date, number, name, place and feeling tied to a moment must come from the fact files. Flourish may reorder, frame and interpret facts. It may not add them. No invented anecdotes, dialogue, weather, sensations or outcomes. If one real detail would help, leave a short placeholder for Karan.
2. **Confidence, no arrogance.** Plain statements with real scope. No self-praise labels (passionate, visionary, natural leader, gifted, exceptional, uniquely positioned). No "mastery". No claims about changing Indian cinema, the industry or the world.
3. **No clichés or AI tells.** Avoid: delve, tapestry, testament, journey (as a metaphor), passion or passionate, navigate (figurative), foster, leverage, robust, pivotal, crucial, realm, landscape (figurative; real landscapes are fine), "not just X but Y", "it's not about X, it's about Y", "Growing up,", "ever since I was a child", "I will never forget", "everything changed", "true calling", "blessing and a curse".
4. **No em dashes** anywhere in essay prose. Use commas, colons or full stops.
5. **Never a production claim.** Karan has not worked in a production department. On Khauf and on the project under Boney Kapoor he was a camera assistant. Behind-the-scenes photography is not production work either. Flourish must not blur this.
6. Format rules, word limits, British spelling, same-university overlap rules and counsellor-copy rules are unchanged.

What changes: longer and more varied sentences are now welcome. One extended image (the frame) may run through an essay. Scene-setting is allowed when every detail in it is true. An essay may end on an aspirational sentence, as long as it rests on his real plan (the studio of 8 to 30 people).

## 3. Karan's through-line: the frame

Karan has his own concept, in his own words, so nothing needs to be imposed. Use it the way the sample uses cultural capital.

**Stage 1, the "contrast frame" (from 2016, on a phone).** He edited every picture from family trips and had them printed. He moved from filters and adjustments to cutouts, new backgrounds and "highlighting the focus of my images in an exaggerated stylish way". The aim was images that stand out.

**Stage 2, the "symmetrical and balanced frame" (landscapes, with a camera).** On trips to Dehradun (from 2017/2018) and to Monaco: long exposures of a changing sky, macro, manual HDR blending, seagulls at the far end of a zoom, an F1 car shot from above in the dead centre of the frame. The aim was composition and control.

**Stage 3, the "impact frame" (people and meaning).** At the Raghu Rai Center for Photography he hid in a corner of a commercial shoot to find frames of his own. In his words, he came to want "a meaningful frame rather than a contrast frame". In France he photographed strangers and his own family "like they're shot on black and white film". He now prefers candids "because now the images bring smiles".

**Then the frame starts to move.** Nitin Rai saw that Karan loved "a free moving camera" more than fixed studio set-ups. The musician's shoot went from stills, to a sequence of pictures with the subject held steady while the background moves, to a shoot that was all video. The Classroom was his first real video work, and he also organised it. At Whistling Woods he trained as a cinematographer.

**Then the camera turns round (Whistling Woods BTS).** Student sets wanted him as their behind-the-scenes photographer, for stills and video. More sets asked than he could cover, so he committed to shoots in advance. On days without a set of his own he still wanted to be on one. He loved beautiful sets, people at work and candid moments. This is the bridge to producing: his camera was no longer pointed at the film but at the people making it.

**Where it lands: producing.** As a camera assistant, he has seen that the problems reaching the camera started earlier (the opening idea of his current UCLA Statement of Purpose). A producer decides what reaches the frame before anyone rolls. That is the skill he wants from the programme.

### How to use it
- Name the idea once, early, in his words. Bring it back once in the middle and once at the close. Do not explain it as a lesson.
- Keep it short in any one essay: two to four sentences across the whole piece. `usage_rule.md` still governs: reference the photography story rather than retell it, use at most **one** concrete moment per essay, no gear lists, no chronology dump.
- Give each essay a different concrete moment. Within UCLA, a sensible split: the **Statement of Purpose** takes the frame idea plus the BTS work, which leads most directly to producing. The **Personal Statement** can take the family-prints beginning and the fact that nobody in his family works in film. Don't reuse a distinctive sentence across sibling applications at the same university.

### Identity framing for Karan
His version of the sample's identity tension, using only true facts:
- Nobody in his family works in film or photography (already in his UCLA Personal Statement).
- He came from a commerce classroom (economics, business studies, accountancy at Suncity School).
- His family "never exactly knew this would become a career", and work was not discussed at home until his senior years at school.
- Before any of that, he was the one in the family who edited and printed every trip photograph. Today his family's homes are full of his pictures.

The tension is real and gentle: a career his family did not see coming, grown out of something they already had on their walls. Play it calmly. No hardship narrative.

### Mapping the sample's arc onto the UCLA Statement of Purpose (500 words; now 443)
1. Identity and frame (about 80 words). A commerce student from a family with no one in film, whose eye began with family photographs. Name the contrast, balanced and impact frames.
2. Method (about 70 words). Why commerce plus the camera matters for producing: he reads a set as a cinematographer and a budget as a commerce student. Keep this plain and true.
3. Origin of the moving image (about 60 words). The Classroom, organised as well as shot and edited.
4. Proof (about 130 words, the densest paragraph). Whistling Woods (B.Sc., TISS), BTS photography for student sets, assistant cinematographer on five short films, Khauf under Pankaj Kumar at Matchbox Studios, the Finolex direction team for Early Man Productions, Sleepyhead planning for Growth Jockey, camera assistant under Boney Kapoor. Use named roles and progressions.
5. Calling and gap (about 50 words). He has not worked in a production office. Development is the interest.
6. Mission (about 90 words). The Producers Program's slate and thesis plan, then the studio in India of 8 to 30 people. Close by bringing the frame back.

Stay under 500. Do not pad.

## 4. Vivid details: which to use, which need care

### Strongest (use freely, one per essay)
- **BTS photographer at Whistling Woods** (Karan, 11 October). The best single fact for a producing essay. Suggested wording: "student sets kept asking me to shoot their behind-the-scenes stills and video, more than I could cover, so I committed to shoots in advance". He also wanted to be on a set even on days he had none of his own (his voice-typed "would not want to work" means "would want to work"). Say "many sets" or "more sets than I could cover". Do not say "every set at the school": he says himself that he could not go to every set. No invented numbers of sets, film titles or anecdotes.
- **Hiding in a corner of the commercial shoot** at the Raghu Rai Center for Photography, looking for better frames than the studio set-up, while Nitin Rai shot the model he had brought in. Both came away with good frames ("so did he": keep that generosity).
- **The friend who modelled for him**, a complete amateur, in the last month of the course: three outfits in two hours (the self fashion shoot of 7–8 March 2020).
- **The musician's shoot.** A secluded hilly road behind the city. Both were happy with the result, and the next day they shot a picture sequence where the subject stays still and the background moves. The final shoot was all video: him driving in, opening the back of the car, a graphic title. Do not name the musician.
- **Editing and printing every family-trip photograph** from 2016.
- **France: developing colour profiles before travelling,** then never feeling the need to edit. Photographing his family as if on black-and-white film. Do not date the trip, and do not say it was "the very next day" after the SHLAY shoot: the records argue against that.
- **The family's homes covered in his pictures,** and his preference for candids "because now the images bring smiles".
- **His own slightly rueful line** that after the course he was "probably way too confident". Used as gentle self-awareness, this works against arrogance.

### Needs care
- **The F1 speed.** He first wrote "about 340 km/h". That is impossible at Monaco, where the 2019 race's top speed-trap reading was 291.9 km/h. He now says it "might be actually 250". 250 km/h is plausible there, but Karan is not sure of it, so **leave out the number**. Write "an F1 car flat out on the straight, shot from above, dead centre in the frame". If Karan later confirms 250 km/h (for example from the photo's EXIF data or the shot location) and wants it in, "about 250 km/h" is physically possible. Do not name a Grand Prix or a year: the trip date is unconfirmed and probably not 2019.
- **The Huawei phone.** Not the P40, which was announced in March 2020, never had Google services, and does not fit a 2016 to 2018 story. Karan now says it was probably the **P20**. The quad-Bayer camera he describes fits the **P20 Pro** (March 2018). Best practice: leave out the model and just say "my phone". If a model must appear, write "Huawei P20 Pro". That dates the phone to 2018, so do not tie it to the 2016 editing. Leave out the US sanctions entirely; they are off-topic. A camera or phone model may appear at most once in a whole application, and only if it carries meaning.
- **"Complete mastery" and "complete confidence".** These read as arrogant, and no 18-year-old has complete mastery of a camera. Use "I taught myself the camera setting by setting", "I learned manual focus for the first time" or "I learned what the 7D could and could not do".
- **"It was the camera, not me, until I handed it to someone else".** This is close to boasting. Use only if turned into the plain lesson in his own words, that knowledge, not the gear, made the difference. Better left out.
- **Raghu Rai's death.** Verified: he died in New Delhi on 26 April 2026, aged 83 (Magnum Photos, PetaPixel and others). **Nitin Rai, his son, is alive**: never imply otherwise. The death is not Karan's story, and using it for emotional weight would read as borrowed grief. At most one factual clause in one essay ("the late Raghu Rai"), only where the Center is already named. In most essays, leave it out.
- **The school's name.** Write "Raghu Rai Center for Photography", never "Raghu Rai Academy", and never "prestigious". If founders are mentioned: "founded by Raghu Rai and his son Nitin", not "opened by Raghu himself". The course was "The Art of Fashion and Advertising", July 2019 to March 2020, about eight months, not a year.
- **Which Nitin.** Most classes were taught by Nitin Choudhary. Nitin Rai led masterclasses and portfolio reviews. Karan himself wrote in 2020 that he "studied directly under Nitin Rai", and the UCLA CV says "under Nitin Rai". Keep that wording, but do not inflate it into a long one-to-one mentorship. Karan should confirm which Nitin the corner-of-the-shoot story is about before it is used.
- **People and brands.** Use "SHLAY" or "Shlay" for the brand. Do not name its founder, the model, the musician, his sister or his cousins without Karan's OK.
- **Filmora.** "My failed stint with Filmora" is his own memory and cannot be checked. It is fine as a light, humble aside.
- **Gear and jargon.** Canon 600D, 7D Mark II, nifty fifty, 18–135mm, Nikon Z6, 24–70mm, IBIS and Gaussian splats are all accurate but are gear lists. Leave them out, except perhaps one model in one essay, if it means something.

## 5. Example of the target voice (EXAMPLE ONLY, not for submission)

The paragraph below is invented to show rhythm and framing. Every fact in it comes from Karan's files, but its wording, order and interpretation are illustrative. Writers must not paste it into an essay, and it must not appear in more than one application. Before any of it is used, confirm with Karan which Nitin the corner-of-the-shoot moment involves.

> *Example:* Before I owned a camera, I was the one in my family who edited every photograph from our trips, cutting out subjects and blending in new backgrounds on my phone until each picture looked louder than the moment had been, and then having them all printed. Those were contrast frames: I wanted images that stood out, and I had not yet asked what they said. Trips with a camera taught me balance, in landscapes, in long exposures of a changing sky, and in seagulls held at the far end of a zoom. It was not until I found myself hiding in a corner of a commercial shoot at the Raghu Rai Center for Photography, looking for frames of my own while Nitin Rai photographed the model he had brought in, that I understood the pictures I cared about were about people. At Whistling Woods International that instinct found work. Student sets asked me to shoot their behind-the-scenes stills and video, more of them than I could cover, so I committed to shoots in advance, and on days without a set of my own I still wanted to be on one. My camera was no longer pointed at the film, but at the people making it.

(About 210 words. In a real essay this would be cut to fit its share of the word limit.)

What it shows: a concessive, flowing opening; the frame idea named in his words; a delayed realisation ("It was not until... that I understood"); one concrete moment, plus the BTS proof; a modest, true scale ("more of them than I could cover"); a closing turn that sets up producing without claiming it. No em dashes, no gear, no numbers that are in doubt, no claim of mastery.

## 6. Checklist before handing back an essay

- [ ] One framing idea (the frame), named early, returned to at the close.
- [ ] Arc: identity, then method, then origin, then proof, then gap, then mission.
- [ ] Proof paragraph names real employers, people and roles from the fact files.
- [ ] At most one concrete photography moment; different from sibling applications.
- [ ] No 340 or 250 km/h, no P40, no "mastery", no "Academy", no "prestigious", no gear list.
- [ ] Raghu Rai's death at most one factual clause, or absent. Nitin Rai never described as dead.
- [ ] Nothing implies he worked in a production department.
- [ ] No em dashes. No words from the banned list. No sample phrasing.
- [ ] Within the word limit; count line updated with `tools/count.py`.
