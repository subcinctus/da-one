1. Narrative Statement

One statement covering: (1) Personal story, the majority: your unique story and what shaped you; what stories you want to tell and why; how your life informed those choices; who you are outside your resume. (2) Discipline of interest: why producing, and the people, movies or art that inspire you in that field. (3) Collaboration experience. (4) Long-term goals and how AFI benefits them.

Nobody in my family works in film, and my parents did not talk to us about work until my senior years at school, so no one at home saw a career coming. It began, as it did for many boys in 2016, with an obsession with phones and their cameras. My editing went from filters and adjustments to graphic elements, and the first thing I taught myself was object selection: cutting a subject out, blending in a new background and highlighting the focus of a picture in an exaggerated, stylish way. I edited every photograph from our family trips and had every one printed. They were contrast frames, built to stand out.

A camera taught me balance. On trips to Dehradun I shot with a Canon 600D and its kit lens, then traded it in for a nifty fifty on a 7D Mark II, a pairing that opened possibilities beyond what a better camera could offer. With it I used manual focus creatively for the first time, played with bokeh, and asked why my camera had no in-body stabilisation when Sony offered it for less. On a family trip to Monaco I taught myself macro, manual HDR blending and long exposures of a changing sky. From above, I caught an F1 car flat out on the straight, dead centre in the frame, and at the far end of an 18-135mm, seagulls soaring about fifty metres away. Those were balanced frames, all composition and control.

The day before I left for Europe, I did a professional shoot for SHLAY, a senior's new boutique clothing brand. I had been sure my Nikon Z6 and its heavy 24-70 were doing the work, until I handed them to someone else and saw that knowledge made the difference. On that shoot balance was no longer enough. I wanted a frame that meant something: an impact frame.

By then my frames had begun to move. The Classroom, a five-part web series I made with friends at Suncity School, was my first real video work: I co-directed, shot, edited and organised it, fitting shoots around our exams. One shoot day an actor dropped out at the last second. We invented a funny sports teacher on the spot, and I played him myself. Our scriptwriter was at home, so we rewrote the lines together remotely to suit him, and I found a standard tracksuit that cost us nothing. The story of the episode still held together. None of us called it producing.

At Whistling Woods International, where I trained as a cinematographer, my camera turned round. Student sets kept asking me to shoot their behind-the-scenes stills and video, more of them than I could cover, so I committed to shoots in advance, and on days with no set of my own I still wanted to be on one, for the beautiful sets, the people at work and the candid moments. I was photographing the people making films, not the films, and watching every department at work. That is what turned me towards producing. My CV lists the sets I have worked on, and as a camera assistant I keep seeing problems reach the camera that began earlier, in a schedule, a budget or a handover. I want to be where those decisions are made. The Lunchbox, which I wish I had produced, and Guneet Monga, who did, have short answers of their own.

The stories I want to produce are about work, money and family, often crossing between India and the United States. REGISTERED POST, the feature I pitch here, is one of them: a family register, an urn posted from Ohio and a young priest in Haridwar caught between the two. I want to tell stories like it with humour and without pity, for audiences in India and abroad.

After AFI I plan to work in development and production for a few years, then build a studio in India of 8 to 30 people with its own slate, giving talented people from outside film families paid work and someone to show them how it is done. AFI teaches the way I learned, by doing, and I want to leave with a thesis film carried from pre-production through post and with Fellows I would want to keep working with. I began with frames built to stand out, then learned balance, then meaning. The frame I want to learn at AFI is the one decided before anyone rolls, so that in my studio, people who start as I did, with a camera and no contacts, can decide it too.

2. Project Pitch

A project you would like to produce as a feature or TV series (original or adapted from a novel, play, news article, current or historical event): 1) concise plot overview with main character arcs; 2) central themes and visual tone; 3) why the story matters to you and why it will connect with an audience.

REGISTERED POST: an original feature film (dramedy, Hindi, Punjabi and English)

1) Plot overview with main character arcs

Haridwar, 4 a.m. Vasu Kaushik (26) stands knee-deep in the Ganga at Har Ki Pauri, a stranger's ashes in one hand and a selfie stick in the other, so a family in New Jersey can watch on WhatsApp from their sofa, where it is still yesterday.

For seven generations his family has kept handwritten registers for villages in Punjab's Doaba belt: births, marriages and deaths, filed by district and village in steel trunks. The villages have emptied into Brampton, Southall and California; the dead now come home by post. Vasu digitises the registers at night and is saving for a master's in America; if he goes, no one is left to carry the trunks.

A brass urn arrives from Dayton, Ohio, by Priority Mail Express International, customs form CREMATED REMAINS, with one instruction in a shaky hand: don't put me in the river until you write me back into the ledger your grandfather struck me from. In the Hoshiarpur trunk Vasu finds the 1974 entry: Darshan Lal Saini, struck through in his grandfather's hand, and in the margin, "Gone to America with the American."

The American is Gene Becker (80), Darshan's widower, a retired machinist who made the urn himself. He came to the Doaba with the Peace Corps in 1972, still speaks the village Punjabi of 1974, and waits on WhatsApp, politely.

Vasu, who has not told his father about America, stalls. Darshan's nephews are the firm's best-paying clients, and in ten days they arrive from Brampton with their father's ashes, to sign the same page. Vasu's father, Pandit Ramesh Kaushik (58), says the ledger is not theirs to edit. He does not mention that in 1974, aged six, he held the blotting paper.

To protect the firm, Ramesh tells the nephews what the urn asks. Surinder (54) is selling the family land; a new name in a register that courts have accepted as evidence could hold up the sale, and he assumes the American wants a share. His brother Bunty (48), who knew their father wrote to Darshan every month and never said so, backs him. Gene hears this on a call and stops being polite, in a village Punjabi so old the nephews need Vasu to translate the insults. He signs away any claim before a notary and holds the deed up to the camera.

When the nephews sit down to sign, Vasu props his phone on the Hoshiarpur trunk, against his father's word, and calls Ohio. Neither nephew reads Gurmukhi well; Gene does, and reads from fifty years of their father's letters to Darshan, every one addressed to both men. Bunty breaks first and admits he knew. Surinder, out of reasons, declares the entry as head of the family. Vasu writes it: Darshan Lal Saini, and beside him Gene Becker of Dayton, Ohio, jeevan saathi, life companion. Ramesh tells his son he held the blotter in 1974, then blots this line himself.

At 4 a.m. the nephews immerse their uncle beside their father while Gene watches at dusk in Dayton, where it is still yesterday. Vasu leaves for his master's with the registers on a hard drive. His father writes "Gone to America" beside his son's name and does not strike it. On a snowy doorstep in Dayton, Vasu gives Gene the blotter from the signing, both names on it backwards. In the hall mirror they read the right way round.

2) Central themes and visual tone

REGISTERED POST is about who a family allows to count. A register keeps and erases at once: a struck line leaves the name legible and removes the person. The film changes a tradition from inside, by its own rules, and turns the homecoming film around: Darshan comes home as a parcel, asking for belonging, not property.

The two worlds meet on a six-inch screen until the last scene. Haridwar fills the wide frame: aarti flames, and the river and steel trunks lit by phone glow at 4 a.m. Ohio's winter exists only as vertical video: snow on a bird feeder, a kitchen at dusk. Handwriting is the recurring image: a grandfather's strike, a brother's letters, a blotter's mirror. The comedy is in the logistics, never the grief.

3) Why the story matters to me and why it will connect with an audience

Haridwar's priests call their registers bahis, the word for an account book. I studied accountancy at Suncity School, and it has a rule this film keeps: you never rub out an entry; you correct it with a new one. At Mitti to Matka, a lockdown initiative for potters, I handled money their families were counting on. The studio of eight to thirty people I plan to build in India is meant to let people in rather than leave them out, and this film is about the same thing.

It is a festival dramedy for families on both ends of these calls, in the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom and India. The closest comparison is The Farewell, a bilingual family film that went from Sundance to wide release. The budget is low: Haridwar locations, a small cast and a few days in Ohio. Gene is an American co-lead in his eighties, built for an India-US co-production.

3. Short answer 1

What is a film or TV series you wish you could have produced and why?

The Lunchbox. A quiet 2013 Mumbai story about a lunch delivered to the wrong office, it is a film I love for its restraint: the sounds of a kitchen, the train commute, a letter read in silence. It reached audiences around the world because of how it was set up, as a co-production across several countries, with a festival launch and distribution that took a very local film abroad. I wish I had produced it because it is the kind of film I want to make: a specific Indian story, made with care, with the financing and partners in place for it to be seen outside India.

4. Short answer 2

What is an area of producing you feel you need more support to learn and grow into the role?

Development and financing. I picked up a camera only when I got bored of zooming into pictures on my phone. After a failed stint with Filmora, a video editor, the camera felt like a new achievement, and I taught myself until there was nothing on it I had not tried. Producing cannot be learned like that: development starts with someone else's draft, and financing with someone else's money.

In development, I want to learn to work with a writer from the earliest treatment: diagnosing what a draft needs, guiding it through many revisions and knowing when a script is ready, without taking over the writer's voice. In financing, I need to learn how the money for a whole film is put together: equity, pre-sales, co-productions between countries, and where a sales agent and a distributor fit in. I have not yet worked in a production office or taken a project from an idea to full financing.

5. Short answer 3

Is there a producer whose career you admire or aspire to emulate?

Guneet Monga. She came into Indian cinema without a film family, co-founded Sikhya Entertainment, produced films such as The Lunchbox and Masaan, and later won an Academy Award as producer of The Elephant Whisperers. What I admire most is that she brings new filmmakers into the industry along with their stories. That is the kind of producer I want to become, and Sikhya is the closest model I have for the company I want to build in India.

6. Short answer 4

Aside from watching films, how do you spend your free time?

Most of my free time still goes into a camera. On a trip to France I reached a point where I could not stop using it, not because of the architecture and not because of the camera. I shot on autopilot: motion long exposures, frozen water, birds, strangers, my own family as if on black-and-white film. I had built my own colour profiles before travelling, and the boy who once edited every family photograph never felt the need to edit a frame. That trip settled, for me, the difference between a career and something I cannot stop doing. At home, my family's houses are now full of my polished prints. I sometimes miss the amateur auto-exposure moments when everyone was made to smile, but I prefer candids now, because now the images bring smiles.

7. Short answer 5 (optional)

Is there something we should know about you that hasn't already been stated in your application?

Two things. My B.Sc. in Filmmaking, Cinematography and Direction from Whistling Woods International, awarded by the Tata Institute of Social Sciences and completed in 2025, is a three-year degree, the standard length for it in India. And before film school I spent about eight months at the Raghu Rai Center for Photography, founded by the late Raghu Rai and his son Nitin. Nitin Rai had spent months teaching me fixed-camera fashion shoots, but he saw that I loved media creation and a free-moving camera far more. On one assignment, when he had called in a model for a commercial shoot, I hid in a corner trying to get better pictures than the Vogue-style studio set-up. I got some beautiful frames, and so did he. In my last month I invited a friend to model for me. She was a complete amateur, and I covered three outfits in two hours. I left probably way too confident.
