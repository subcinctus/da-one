1. Narrative Statement

One statement covering: (1) Personal story, the majority: your unique story and what shaped you; what stories you want to tell and why; how your life informed those choices; who you are outside your resume. (2) Discipline of interest: why producing, and the people, movies or art that inspire you in that field. (3) Collaboration experience. (4) Long-term goals and how AFI benefits them.

Nobody in my family works in film, and while I studied commerce at Suncity School in Gurugram, no one at home knew a career would come out of my photographs. I was the one who edited every picture from our family trips on my phone, cutting out subjects and blending in new backgrounds until each looked louder than the moment, and had them all printed: contrast frames, made to stand out. Trips with a camera taught me the balanced frame. It was not until I hid in a corner at the Raghu Rai Center for Photography, during a commercial shoot with a model, looking for better frames than the studio set-up allowed, that I understood the pictures I cared about were about people. I came away with good frames, as did the photographer leading the shoot, but afterwards I wanted the impact frame and a camera free to move.

The frame began to move with The Classroom, a five-part web series my friends and I made at school and released on YouTube. I co-directed, shot and edited it, and because nobody else was going to, I found places to shoot, scheduled shoots around our exams and kept classmates involved. None of us called that producing.

During COVID, when Gurugram's potters saw their sales collapse, I joined Mitti to Matka, an initiative that bought and auctioned their pots at fair prices and supplied food and sanitary supplies to their families. As media manager I made the photographs, videos and graphics, ran the website and handled administration. Buyers saw the pots through my pictures, so my work shaped what a family earned, which made me careful with money families relied on and honest about what a small team could do.

The stories I want to produce are about work, money and family, often crossing between India and the United States. REGISTERED POST, the project I pitch here, follows a young priest in Haridwar, saving to emigrate, who immerses ashes mailed from America into the Ganga on WhatsApp video, until an urn from Ohio asks him to write the man inside back into the register his grandfather struck him from. I want to tell stories like his with humour and without pity, for audiences in India and abroad.

Why producing. At Whistling Woods International in Mumbai I completed a B.Sc. in Filmmaking, Cinematography and Direction in 2025, awarded by the Tata Institute of Social Sciences, and worked as assistant cinematographer on five short films and lead or assistant on independent films. There my camera turned round: student sets kept asking me to shoot their behind-the-scenes stills and video, more than I could cover, so I committed to shoots in advance. Behind the camera or photographing the crew, I kept noticing that problems reaching the lens started earlier, in a schedule, a budget or a handoff, and I wanted to be where those decisions are made. The producer I admire most is Guneet Monga, who took The Lunchbox and Masaan to audiences abroad.

Collaboration. I have worked as a camera assistant on Khauf at Matchbox Studios under Pankaj Kumar and, since October 2025, at Bayview Projects under producer Boney Kapoor. On the direction team of a Finolex commercial for Early Man Productions, I prepared the production sheets, cast references and PPM deck and ran costume trials, and for Growth Jockey I planned a Sleepyhead Mattresses shoot: its equipment, script, offer and audience.

One shoot day on The Classroom shows how we worked together. An actor dropped out at the last second. We made up a new role then and there, a funny sports teacher, and I played him. Our scriptwriter was at home, so we rewrote the new role's lines together, remotely. I found a standard tracksuit that cost us nothing, and the episode's story still held together.

Long-term goals. After AFI I plan to work in development and production for a few years, then build a studio of 8 to 30 people in India that develops its own slate and gives talented people from outside film families paid work and someone to show them how it is done. I have not yet worked in a production office and need what the camera department does not teach: developing a project with writers and directors, budgeting and scheduling it, and financing and selling it. I have learned most things by doing them, which is how AFI teaches: cycle films with different Fellows in the first year, then a thesis film from pre-production through post. Until now I have chosen frames from wherever I stood. At AFI I want to learn to decide what reaches the frame long before the camera rolls, and then give people who start as I did, with a camera and no contacts, the chance to make those decisions in my studio.

2. Project Pitch

A project you would like to produce as a feature or TV series (original or adapted from a novel, play, news article, current or historical event): 1) concise plot overview with main character arcs; 2) central themes and visual tone; 3) why the story matters to you and why it will connect with an audience.

REGISTERED POST: an original feature film (dramedy, Hindi, Punjabi and English)

1) Plot overview with main character arcs

Haridwar, 4 a.m. Vasu Kaushik (26) stands knee-deep in the Ganga at Har Ki Pauri, a stranger's ashes in one hand and a selfie stick in the other, so a family in New Jersey can watch on WhatsApp from their sofa, where it is still yesterday.

For seven generations his family has kept handwritten registers for villages in Punjab's Doaba belt: births, marriages and deaths, filed by district and village in steel trunks. The villages have emptied into Brampton, Southall and California; the dead now come home by post. Vasu digitises the registers at night and is saving for a master's in America; if he goes, no one is left to carry the trunks.

A brass urn arrives from Dayton, Ohio, by Priority Mail Express International, customs form CREMATED REMAINS, with one instruction in a shaky hand: don't put me in the river until you write me back into the ledger your grandfather struck me from. In the Hoshiarpur trunk Vasu finds the 1974 entry: Darshan Lal Saini, struck through in his grandfather's hand, and in the margin, "Gone to America with the American."

The American is Gene Becker (80), Darshan's widower, a retired machinist who made the urn himself. He came to the Doaba with the Peace Corps in 1972, still speaks the village Punjabi of 1974, and waits on WhatsApp, politely.

Vasu, who has not told his father about America, stalls. Darshan's nephews are the firm's best-paying clients, and in ten days they arrive from Brampton with their father's ashes, to sign the same page. Vasu's father, Pandit Ramesh Kaushik (58), says the ledger is not theirs to edit. He does not mention that in 1974, aged six, he held the blotting paper.

To protect the firm, Ramesh tells the nephews what the urn asks. Surinder (54) is selling the family land; a new name in a register that courts have accepted as evidence could hold up the sale, and he assumes the American wants a share. His brother Bunty (48), who knew their father wrote to Darshan every month and never said so, backs him. Gene hears this on a call and stops being polite, in a village Punjabi so old the nephews need Vasu to translate the insults. He signs away any claim before a notary and holds the deed up to the camera.

When the nephews sit down to sign, Vasu props his phone on the Hoshiarpur trunk, against his father's word, and calls Ohio. Neither nephew reads Gurmukhi well; Gene does, and reads from fifty years of their father's letters to Darshan, every one addressed to both men. Bunty breaks first and admits he knew. Surinder, out of reasons, declares the entry as head of the family. Vasu writes it: Darshan Lal Saini, and beside him Gene Becker of Dayton, Ohio, jeevan saathi, life companion. Ramesh tells his son he held the blotter in 1974, then blots this line himself.

At 4 a.m. the nephews immerse their uncle beside their father while Gene watches at dusk in Dayton, where it is still yesterday. Vasu leaves for his master's with the registers on a hard drive. His father writes "Gone to America" beside his son's name and does not strike it. On a snowy doorstep in Dayton, Vasu gives Gene the blotter from the signing, both names on it backwards. In the hall mirror they read the right way round.

2) Central themes and visual tone

REGISTERED POST is about who a family allows to count. A register keeps and erases at once: a struck line leaves the name legible and removes the person. The film changes a tradition from inside, by its own rules, and turns the homecoming film around: Darshan comes home as a parcel, asking for belonging, not property.

The two worlds meet on a six-inch screen until the last scene. Haridwar fills the wide frame: aarti flames, and the river and steel trunks lit by phone glow at 4 a.m. Ohio's winter exists only as vertical video: snow on a bird feeder, a kitchen at dusk. Handwriting is the recurring image: a grandfather's strike, a brother's letters, a blotter's mirror. The comedy is in the logistics, never the grief.

3) Why the story matters to me and why it will connect with an audience

Haridwar's priests call their registers bahis, the word for an account book. I studied accountancy at Suncity School, and it has a rule this film keeps: you never rub out an entry; you correct it with a new one. At Mitti to Matka, a lockdown initiative for potters, I handled money their families were counting on. The studio of eight to thirty people I plan to build in India is meant to let people in rather than leave them out, and this film is about the same thing.

It is a festival dramedy for families on both ends of these calls, in the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom and India. The closest comparison is The Farewell, a bilingual family film that went from Sundance to wide release. The budget is low: Haridwar locations, a small cast and a few days in Ohio. Gene is an American co-lead in his eighties, built for an India-US co-production.

3. Short answer 1

What is a film or TV series you wish you could have produced and why?

The Lunchbox (2013). A quiet Mumbai story about a lunch delivered to the wrong office, it is a film I love for its restraint: the sounds of a kitchen, the train commute, a letter read in silence. It reached audiences around the world because of how it was set up, as a co-production across several countries, with a festival launch and distribution that took a very local film abroad. I wish I had produced it because it is the kind of film I want to make: a specific Indian story, made with care, with the financing and partners in place for it to be seen outside India.

4. Short answer 2

What is an area of producing you feel you need more support to learn and grow into the role?

Development and financing. My experience so far is in the camera department, plus behind-the-scenes photography on student sets, direction-team work on a Finolex commercial and the planning of a Sleepyhead Mattresses shoot. I have watched every department on a set at work, but I have not worked in a production office or taken a project from an idea to a fully financed production.

In development, I want to learn to work with a writer from the earliest treatment: diagnosing what a draft needs, guiding it through many revisions, and knowing when a script is ready, without taking over the writer's voice. In financing, I need to learn how money for a whole film is put together: equity, pre-sales, co-productions between countries, and how a sales agent and a distributor fit in. These are the skills I need before I can run a studio of my own, and the hardest to learn alone.

5. Short answer 3

Is there a producer whose career you admire or aspire to emulate?

Guneet Monga. She came into Indian cinema without a film family and built Sikhya Entertainment, producing films such as The Lunchbox and Masaan that reached international audiences, and later won an Academy Award as producer of The Elephant Whisperers. What I admire most is that she connects Indian stories with audiences abroad, and new filmmakers with the industry. That is the kind of producer I want to become, and Sikhya, built by someone who started without connections, is the closest model I have for the studio I plan to build in India.

6. Short answer 4

Aside from watching films, how do you spend your free time?

Most of my free time still goes into a camera. It began with editing and printing every photograph from our family trips, and today my family's homes are covered in my pictures, though the ones I like best now are the candids, because they make people smile. Before a trip to France I developed colour profiles, and once there I photographed strangers, birds and my own family as if on black-and-white film and never felt the need to edit a frame. Even at Whistling Woods, on days without a set of my own, I wanted to be on one, photographing people at work and candid moments.
