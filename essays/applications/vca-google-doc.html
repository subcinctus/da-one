1. Statement of Intent

Reflect on your personal and professional history. What has influenced your professional growth and has contributed to your decision to apply for this University of Melbourne program? Why do you want to be a producer? What are your long-term professional goals (new directions, opportunities, interests, skills, professional renewal, and work advancement), and how do you think this program would help you to meet them?

Nobody in my family works in film or photography, and my parents never exactly knew that the pictures I kept making would become a career. From 2016 I was the one who edited every photograph from our trips on my phone and had them printed, wanting each one to stand out; looking back, I call those contrast frames. Travelling with a camera taught me the balanced frame, built on composition and control, and about eight months of fashion and advertising photography at the Raghu Rai Center for Photography, where I studied under Nitin Rai, left me wanting what I now call the impact frame, a picture that means something to the people in it. Only later did I understand that the most important decisions about any frame are made before a camera is switched on, and that understanding is why I am applying for the Screen Producing specialisation of the Master of Film and Television.

I came to film from a commerce classroom. At Suncity School in Gurugram I studied economics, business studies and accountancy, and the ledger and the camera felt like two separate lives until COVID, when they met. While still at school I became media manager for Mitti to Matka, an initiative supporting Gurugram's potters after their sales collapsed. I made the photographs, videos and graphics, ran the website and handled the administration, and because the money we raised was meant for the potters' families, I learned to be careful with every rupee of it.

The moving image came to me through friends. With classmates I made The Classroom, a five-part web series we released on YouTube. I co-directed, shot and edited it, and I also organised it, finding places to shoot, scheduling shoots around exams and keeping everyone involved. On one shoot day an actor dropped out at the last moment. Rather than lose the day, we invented a new character on the spot, a funny sports teacher, and I played him. Our scriptwriter was at home, so the two of us rewrote the lines remotely, and I found a standard tracksuit for the costume without spending anything. The episode still held together. I did not have the word for it then, but that was my first producing decision: keep the day, protect the story and spend nothing we did not have.

At Whistling Woods International in Mumbai I trained as a cinematographer, completing a B.Sc. in Filmmaking, Cinematography and Direction, awarded by the Tata Institute of Social Sciences, in 2025, and worked as an assistant cinematographer on five short films and as lead or assistant cinematographer on independent films. Student sets also kept asking me to be their behind-the-scenes photographer, for stills and video, more sets than I could cover, so I committed to shoots in advance, and even on days with no set of my own, I wanted to be on one. With my camera turned towards the people making a film, I watched every department at work. Alongside my studies I was a camera assistant on Khauf at Matchbox Studios under Pankaj Kumar, and since October 2025 I have been a camera assistant at Bayview Projects under producer Boney Kapoor. On the direction team of a Finolex commercial for Early Man Productions I prepared the production sheets, cast references and PPM deck and ran costume trials, and for Growth Jockey I planned a Sleepyhead Mattresses shoot, from the equipment and script to the offer and audience the film was aimed at.

From the camera department I have seen, set after set, how decisions made earlier, about the schedule, the budget, the locations and the cast, shape everything that happens in front of the lens. I want to be a producer because a producer makes those decisions, and because the work I have enjoyed most has always been the work around the camera. I have not yet worked in a production office, developed a script with a writer, put financing together, drawn up a full budget or taken a film to market. Those are the skills I am applying to learn.

The structure of this course is why I am applying to the Victorian College of the Arts. As a Screen Producing student I would take part in graduate productions alongside students in the Filmmaking specialisation, in senior production roles on real films, mentored by staff and industry guests. I know a set from the camera side; this course would put me on the other side of the same conversations. In the second year, a producer-led project, individual or collaborative, that I could keep developing for the marketplace after graduating would be the first step towards the slate my own company will need. I have two treatments I want to learn to develop properly: MARRYING THE ICE, a comic drama about a young homestay hustler in Ladakh whose grandmother will not let him leave until their village's dead stream runs again, and BORROWED SKY, an eight-episode period series about an ISRO technician keeping the first televisions in forty Orissa villages alive in 1975. Both need a producer who understands development, financing and distribution as well as the set.

For me this degree is a change of department more than a change of direction. After graduating I want to spend a few years in development and production, then build a studio in India of 8 to 30 people that develops its own films and series, small enough for everyone to know what a decision costs, run on clear budgets and fair contracts, and open to talented people who, like me, had no one in the industry to call. For years I photographed the people who make films; now I want to be responsible for them. If the camera teams on that studio's sets can spend their days looking for the impact frame instead of working around problems that began somewhere else, I will have used this course as I intend to.
