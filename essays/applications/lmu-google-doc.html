School of Film and Television, MFA in Film and Television Production (Creative Producing)

Deadline: 15 November 2026

1. Personal Statement

Tell us your story. How have your life experiences, choices, and values shaped you? Is there a unique challenge in your life that you overcame? What are the unique stories you want to tell as a filmmaker? Tell us about artists (filmmakers or otherwise) and creative influences that have inspired you. Why do you want to attend a film program, and why specifically the Film and Television Production program at LMU? What specialization (directing, producing, cinematography, editing, or documentary) are you most interested in, and why?

Nobody in my family works in film or photography, and while I studied economics, business studies and accountancy at Suncity School in Gurugram, no one at home expected a career to come out of the pictures I kept editing. I was the one in the family who edited every photograph from our trips and had them all printed, cutting out subjects on my phone and blending in new backgrounds until each picture stood out. Those were my contrast frames. Trips with a camera taught me the balanced frame, symmetry and long exposures of a changing sky, and people taught me to want what I call the impact frame, a picture chosen for what it says about the people in it rather than for how it looks. Today my family's homes are full of my photographs, and I like the candid ones best, because they make people smile.

I liked both halves of that life, the balance sheets in class and the light in the studio, where I spent about eight months on a fashion photography course under Nitin Rai at the Raghu Rai Center for Photography. By the end of it I cared more about a camera that moves than about a fixed studio set-up, and I was also, I think now, far too confident.

The frame had already started to move. With a group of friends I made The Classroom, a five-part web series we produced ourselves and released on YouTube. I co-directed, shot and edited it, and without calling it that, I was producing it too: I found the places we shot in, fitted the shoots around our exams and kept classmates involved until all five episodes were done. After the course I asked a musician at my school to shoot with me. We took stills on a secluded hilly road behind the city, liked them enough to make a sequence the next day in which he held still while the background moved, and ended with a shoot that was all video.

In 2020, while I was still at school, the COVID lockdown cost Gurugram's local potters their markets, and I joined Mitti to Matka, an initiative set up to help them. We bought and auctioned their pots at fair prices, worked with them on design improvements and delivered food and sanitary supplies to their families, and as media manager I made the photographs, videos and graphics, ran the website and handled the administration. It was a challenge in two ways. We were young volunteers with little money of our own, and because the money we raised was meant for the families, I was careful with every expense. We could also only do so much: selling pots and delivering supplies could never make up for what the lockdown had cost the potters. I accepted that, kept at the part we could do, and stayed with it until 2021.

ROLL CALL, the short film I outline in this application, is set in a Rajasthan village in 1951, on the day the women behind a purdah screen must say their own names aloud so that India's first electoral roll can count them, and a drill master has until sunset to make it happen. I am drawn to moments when something as large as an election comes down to a few people in one place on one day, and I want to tell stories like that from India, with humour and without pity, because I rarely see the people in them on screen.

In Raghu Rai's photographs of India, one frame holds a whole street. The Lunchbox, a Mumbai film about a lunch delivered to the wrong office, showed me what good producing can do for a quiet story: it was built as an international co-production and released around the world. One of its producers, Guneet Monga, came into Indian cinema without a film family and built a company that takes Indian stories to audiences abroad, and hers is a career I study.

At Whistling Woods International in Mumbai I completed a B.Sc. in Filmmaking, Cinematography and Direction in 2025, with the degree conferred by the Tata Institute of Social Sciences, working as assistant cinematographer on five short films and as lead or assistant cinematographer on independent films. There my camera turned round, from the film to the people making it: student sets wanted me as their behind-the-scenes photographer, for stills and video, more of them than I could get to, so I committed to shoots in advance, and even on days without a set of my own I wanted to be on one. In the industry I have mostly worked in the camera department: on Khauf, for Matchbox Studios, I was a camera assistant under Pankaj Kumar, and since October 2025 I have done the same job at Bayview Projects under producer Boney Kapoor. On a Finolex commercial for Early Man Productions, working on the direction team, I prepared the production sheets, cast references and PPM deck and ran the costume trials, and for Growth Jockey I planned a Sleepyhead Mattresses shoot: its equipment, script, offer and audience.

None of these jobs was in a production department, and I have not yet worked on a professional project while it was being developed and financed, or seen one through packaging and release. That is what I want a film programme for: to learn development, financing, budgeting, scheduling and distribution while making films with directors, writers and cinematographers I hope to keep working with after the degree.

At LMU everyone first takes a common core and learns every role, and only then do producers specialise, which suits someone coming to producing from the camera. In Creative Producing I would work with directing and screenwriting students, learn to develop, sell and monetise digital content, and finish with a thesis that combines produced work with a series bible, pitch materials and a business and marketing strategy, the package I will need for every project I take on. The required internship and professional mentor would tie the training to the industry in Los Angeles. I also like that LMU asks filmmakers to think about the people their work affects. I first had to do that at Mitti to Matka, where my photographs helped decide what a family earned.

I want to specialise in producing. I value my training in cinematography and direction, but on most sets I have worked on, the important decisions were made before the camera rolled. A producer decides which stories get made, who makes them and how the money is spent, and I want to make those decisions well, helped by knowing what a scene takes to shoot and how to talk to a crew. After a few years in development and production, I plan to build a creative studio in India of 8 to 30 people that develops its own slate, pays people fairly and trains them on the job. My first frames were meant to stand out, and the ones I like best now are about the people in them. If that studio can make a few more films about the people I rarely see on screen, that will be the impact frame I have been looking for.

2. Short-format film outline

An outline of a short-format film project you would like to create. It should give a sense of the stories you want to tell and the genres you want to work in.

Title: ROLL CALL

Genre: Comedy-drama, 12 minutes, in Hindi and Rajasthani

Logline: Rajasthan, 1951: a bellowing school drill master has until sunset to get twenty women behind a purdah screen to say their own names for India's first electoral roll, but the eldest has forgotten hers, so the women begin choosing new ones.

Outline: The Saraf men trade in Calcutta; their women run the painted Shekhawati haveli. Bhanwar Lal, drill master, whistle and all, must enrol twenty by sunset on the last day. His register lists only "wife of" and "mother of", every line struck through in red. He sits with his back to a carved stone jaali.

His roll call gets laughter. Then Badi Bahu sets terms: his wife's name first. He has only ever called her Munna's mother. He bellows "Kesar!" at the wall. Nobody laughs. Badi Bahu gives hers, Champa, and fourteen more follow. Dadi-sa, eighty, has forgotten hers, so the youngest bride goes first: Bijli, lightning. He writes it and pencils his own rule in the margin: a name is what she answers to. A widow chooses Azadi, freedom. The last two choose Calcutta and Bhanwari (after him); Dhapu, whose name means Enough, trades hers for Maharani.

At sunset, as the tehsil peon shouts from the gate, he calls the roll without the whistle. Nineteen voices answer "Haazir" through the stone. On line twenty he waits. Far off, a woman calls a child in, and Dadi-sa remembers the name her mother called at dusk: Gaura. Faces bare on the roof, the women watch the peon's lantern carry their names away. Closing card: nearly 2.8 million women were struck off India's first electoral rolls because they would not give their own names.

Tone and look: One room, one afternoon. The clock is the jaali's star-shaped light, climbing the painted wall past a motorcar into a train of Englishmen. The camera sees the women; he never does. This is the kind of story I want to make: a comedy about who gets counted.

3. The Unforgettable Moment

Describe a truly unforgettable moment you either experienced or witnessed.

The moment I come back to most happened on a shoot day for The Classroom, the web series my friends and I made at school. I organised it, so the shoot days, fitted into the gaps between our exams, were mine to arrange, and on one of them an actor dropped out at the last second.

We did not want to give the day up, so we changed the episode instead, and there and then we came up with a new character to fill the missing role: a funny sports teacher. Someone had to play him, and I did.

The scene had not been written for him. Every line belonged to the character we had lost, and our scriptwriter was at home, not with us, so the two of us rewrote the lines for the new role remotely. A sports teacher also has to look like one, and I got a standard tracksuit that cost us nothing.

We kept the shoot day, and the episode's story still held together. It was only later, on professional sets as a camera assistant, that I understood what that day had been: changing the plan, rewriting the scene and costuming a new role without spending anything is a producer's work, and it is the work I want to train for at LMU.

1. Personal Statement

Why you are pursuing a Master in Entertainment Leadership and Management, the reasons you are applying to LMU, and the unique strengths you will bring. Also describe how you have approached diversity, equity and inclusion in your professional and/or personal life.

I came to film from a commerce classroom in Gurugram and a home where no one worked in the industry. My first pictures were edited on a phone until they stood out, contrast frames, as I call them now. It was not until I hid in a corner of a commercial shoot at the Raghu Rai Center for Photography, trying for a better picture of the model than the studio set-up allowed, that I began to want an impact frame, one built around people rather than style.

On sets since then, I have seen that most of what reaches the frame is settled earlier, in budget, schedule and casting. At Whistling Woods International, Mumbai, where I trained in cinematography and direction and completed a TISS degree in 2025, student sets kept asking me to shoot their behind-the-scenes stills and video, more than I could cover, and on those sets I watched every department at work. Since October 2025 I have been a camera assistant at Bayview Projects, under producer Boney Kapoor, as I was on Khauf for Matchbox Studios. For Early Man Productions I was on the direction team of a Finolex commercial, preparing the pre-production meeting deck, production sheets and cast references and running the costume trials, and the Sleepyhead Mattresses shoot I planned for Growth Jockey covered script, kit, offer and audience. Before all this, I organised The Classroom, a five-episode YouTube series with school friends, finding locations and scheduling shoots around exams.

I have not yet worked in a production office, set a budget or financed a project. The studio I mean to found in India after some years in development and production, 8 to 30 people making their own films and series, will need finance, strategy and management skills my film training left out. MELM, run jointly with the School of Film and Television, keeps business teaching close to creative work, and its entertainment accounting and finance, producing master class and new-venture courses cover what I lack. The Entrepreneurial Producer capstone would let me pitch and launch a venture before I graduate. I would bring set experience, a cinematographer's sense of what things cost on screen, and accountancy from school.

My approach to diversity, equity and inclusion began at Mitti to Matka, which I joined at school when the COVID lockdown took away the markets of Gurugram's potters. Fair prices for the makers came first: we bought their pots to auction, worked with the potters on better designs and carried food and sanitary supplies to the families, and as its media manager I ran the website and administration as well as the stills, videos and graphics. Our auctions could never replace the markets they had lost, and there I saw where help like ours stops. Entering the industry afterwards without connections, often as the newest person on set, taught me to notice who gets a first chance.

I want to run my studio on the same terms: honest numbers, clear agreements, hiring on ability, and time to teach the newest person on the team. Contrast frames were about being noticed. My studio should be judged by its impact frames: films that mean something, made by people who got their first chance there. That is the company I want LMU to teach me to run.
