School of Film and Television, MFA in Film and Television Production (Creative Producing)

Deadline: 15 November 2026

1. Personal Statement

Tell us your story. How have your life experiences, choices, and values shaped you? Is there a unique challenge in your life that you overcame? What are the unique stories you want to tell as a filmmaker? Tell us about artists (filmmakers or otherwise) and creative influences that have inspired you. Why do you want to attend a film program, and why specifically the Film and Television Production program at LMU? What specialization (directing, producing, cinematography, editing, or documentary) are you most interested in, and why?

A phone trained my eye. In 2016 I was obsessed, as many boys my age were, with phones and their cameras, Huawei's above all, and I edited every photograph from my family's trips, moving from filters and adjustments to graphic elements. The first thing I taught myself was object selection: lifting a subject out, blending in a new background and highlighting the focus in an exaggerated, stylish way. Then I had them all printed. Those were contrast frames, made to shout, and it would be years before I asked what they were saying.

Only when I grew bored of zooming into pictures on a screen did I pick up a camera. After my failed stint with Filmora, a video editor, the camera felt like a new achievement, and I kept at it until there was nothing on it I had not tried. On trips to Dehradun, where my sibling and two cousins were at school, I shot on a Canon 600D with its kit lens, then traded it in for a nifty fifty on a 7D Mark II, a pairing that opened possibilities beyond what a better camera could offer. With it I used manual focus creatively for the first time and played with bokeh.

In Monaco, with my family, I found the balanced frame, teaching myself macro, manual HDR blending and long exposures of a changing sky. From above, I caught an F1 car flat out on the straight, centred exactly, and pushed an 18-135mm to full zoom for seagulls wheeling some fifty metres off.

My first real video work was The Classroom, a web series of five episodes my friends and I made at Suncity School. I co-directed, shot, edited and organised it, and learned that a series needs more than a camera: it needs someone to hold it together. After it I had confidence.

The Raghu Rai Center for Photography meant about eight months under Nitin Rai, much of it spent on fashion shoots with a fixed camera. On one assignment he called in a model for a commercial shoot, and I tucked myself into a corner of the studio, chasing pictures that could beat the Vogue-style set-up. I came away with some beautiful frames, and so did he, and it was then that Nitin saw how much more I loved a camera free to move. In my last month I invited a friend to model for me; she was a complete amateur, and we covered three outfits in two hours. I left, I admit, probably way too confident.

I took that confidence to a schoolmate whose music had blown my mind in 11th grade, a musician then making things for fun like me and now steadily releasing and producing. We shot many, many pictures on a secluded hilly road outside the city, and the very next day, at his request, a sequence built from stills, the musician fixed in place while everything behind him slid past. He loved it so much he would not risk a new location, so we went back once more, and this time it was all video: him driving in, opening the back of the car, a graphic title at the top. That was where my stills began to move.

By the time SHLAY, a senior's new boutique clothing brand, asked me for a professional shoot, I had moved to a Nikon Z6 with a heavy 24-70, and what I wanted from it had changed: not a contrast frame, not a balanced one, but a frame with meaning, an impact frame.

The day after the shoot I left for Europe, and in France I reached a point where I could not stop using the camera, not because of the architecture, not because of the camera. I ran on autopilot: motion in long exposures, frozen water, birds, strangers, my own family as if on black-and-white film. I had built colour profiles before travelling and never felt the need to edit a frame. That trip settled, for me, the difference between loving the camera and building a career on it.

Making that choice was the challenge I had to overcome, a quiet one. My parents did not raise work with us until my last years of school, my own subjects were economics, business studies and accountancy, and nobody at home saw this coming. I found my own way in, and today my family's houses are full of my polished prints.

At Whistling Woods International, where I trained as a cinematographer, student crews began asking me to photograph and film behind the scenes on their sets, more crews than I could reach, so I gave my word in advance. The camera Nitin had noticed, the one that would not stay still, had found its subject: crews at work, on sets that were not my own. My portfolio list records the films I have shot. As a camera assistant since, I keep seeing problems reach the camera that began with decisions made long before anyone called action, at a desk rather than on the floor, and I have come to want the desk. None of my work has yet been in a production department, which is why I want a film programme: to learn development, financing, budgeting, scheduling and distribution.

The stories I want to tell look like ROLL CALL, my outline in this application: something as large as India's first electoral roll coming down to one room and one afternoon, told with humour and without pity, about people I rarely see on screen. My first influence was the late Raghu Rai, whose single frames can hold a whole street.

LMU's common core teaches every role before producers specialise, which is how I came to producing too: for years my camera followed each department on a set. In Creative Producing I would work with directing and screenwriting students, learn to develop, sell and monetise digital content, and finish with a thesis that pairs produced work with a series bible, pitch materials and a business and marketing strategy. The required internship and professional mentor would tie it to the industry in Los Angeles, and Nitin Rai showed me what one experienced eye can see in a student.

I want to specialise in producing, where the frame is decided: which stories get made, who makes them and how the money is spent. I know what a scene takes to shoot, not yet what it takes to get made. After a few years in development and production, I plan to build a studio in India of 8 to 30 people that develops its own slate, pays people fairly and trains them on the job. My first prints were made to shout. The studio's films should do something harder: hold the people usually left outside the frame. That is the impact frame I have been working towards.

2. Short-format film outline

An outline of a short-format film project you would like to create. It should give a sense of the stories you want to tell and the genres you want to work in.

Title: ROLL CALL

Genre: Comedy-drama, 12 minutes, in Hindi and Rajasthani

Logline: Rajasthan, 1951: a bellowing school drill master has until sunset to get twenty women behind a purdah screen to say their own names for India's first electoral roll, but the eldest has forgotten hers, so the women begin choosing new ones.

Outline: The Saraf men trade in Calcutta; their women run the painted Shekhawati haveli. Bhanwar Lal, drill master, whistle and all, must enrol twenty by sunset on the last day. His register lists only "wife of" and "mother of", every line struck through in red. He sits with his back to a carved stone jaali.

His roll call gets laughter. Then Badi Bahu sets terms: his wife's name first. He has only ever called her Munna's mother. He bellows "Kesar!" at the wall. Nobody laughs. Badi Bahu gives hers, Champa, and fourteen more follow. Dadi-sa, eighty, has forgotten hers, so the youngest bride goes first: Bijli, lightning. He writes it and pencils his own rule in the margin: a name is what she answers to. A widow chooses Azadi, freedom. The last two choose Calcutta and Bhanwari (after him); Dhapu, whose name means Enough, trades hers for Maharani.

At sunset, as the tehsil peon shouts from the gate, he calls the roll without the whistle. Nineteen voices answer "Haazir" through the stone. On line twenty he waits. Far off, a woman calls a child in, and Dadi-sa remembers the name her mother called at dusk: Gaura. Faces bare on the roof, the women watch the peon's lantern carry their names away. Closing card: nearly 2.8 million women were struck off India's first electoral rolls because they would not give their own names.

Tone and look: One room, one afternoon. The clock is the jaali's star-shaped light, climbing the painted wall past a motorcar into a train of Englishmen. The camera sees the women; he never does. This is the kind of story I want to make: a comedy about who gets counted.

3. The Unforgettable Moment

Describe a truly unforgettable moment you either experienced or witnessed.

The moment I return to most came on a shoot day for The Classroom, the web series my friends and I made at school, when an actor playing one of the roles dropped out at the last second.

Losing the day would have been the easy answer. We decided against it, and on the spot we invented a new character to take the place of the missing role: a funny sports teacher. Somebody had to play him, and it had to be me. That is the moment I keep: the person who usually shot our episodes stepping in front of the camera.

That solved one problem and opened two more. The lines had been written for the role we had lost, and our scriptwriter was at home, not on set, so we rewrote them together remotely until they suited a sports teacher. He also had to look like one, and I managed to get a standard tracksuit at no cost. We kept the day, and the story of the episode still held together.

Only later, on professional sets, did I understand what that day had been. When the plan failed, we changed what could still change, a role, its lines and its costume, and spent nothing. At the time I had no name for that. Now I do: producing, and it is the work I want to train for at LMU.

College of Business Administration, Master in Entertainment Leadership and Management (MELM)

1. Personal Statement

Why you are pursuing a Master in Entertainment Leadership and Management, the reasons you are applying to LMU, and the unique strengths you will bring. Also describe how you have approached diversity, equity and inclusion in your professional and/or personal life.

I came to film from a commerce classroom in Gurugram, but my first audience was my family. From 2016, every trip we took came home through my phone: I cut people out of their surroundings, set them against new ones and pushed whatever mattered to the front until nobody could miss it, then sent every one to print. I think of them now as contrast frames, pictures that won attention before they had anything to say.

The camera that followed taught me value before it taught me art. By my second Canon I was asking why it lacked in-body stabilisation when Sony offered it for less: my first question about what a price actually buys. Landscapes then trained me in balance: symmetry, long exposures, everything placed.

The impact frame came with my first professional brief. SHLAY, a senior's newly launched boutique clothing label, asked me to shoot for it, and for the first time my pictures had to work for someone else's venture. Loud and level were no longer enough; I wanted frames that meant something.

A camera that moves follows people; at Whistling Woods International it followed the crews. Student sets wanted my behind-the-scenes stills and video faster than I could say yes, so I booked myself in advance. Photographing people at work, rather than the film, showed me where a production lives. As a camera assistant on sets my CV lists, I stand where decisions land: trouble at the camera usually begins in the call sheet, the budget or the casting.

My approach to diversity, equity and inclusion began at Mitti to Matka, which I joined at school when the COVID lockdown cost Gurugram's potters their markets. We paid the makers fair prices for pots to auction, carried food and sanitary supplies to their families, and I ran our media, website and administration. Our auctions could never replace those markets, and I saw where help like ours stops: fairness has to be built into how money moves.

I have not yet learned how money moves: no budget set, no project financed, not a day in a production office. MELM, run jointly with the School of Film and Television, keeps business teaching close to creative work; its entertainment accounting and finance, producing master class and new-venture courses cover those gaps, and the Entrepreneurial Producer capstone would let me pitch and launch a venture before I graduate. I would bring a cinematographer's sense of what things cost on screen and a commerce student's habit of asking what they cost off it.

Contrast frames taught me how to get noticed, balanced ones to be precise and the impact frame what is worth making. The studio I plan to found in India, 8 to 30 people making their own films and series, will need all three, and someone to balance its books as carefully as its frames. LMU is where I want to learn to run it; home is where I will build it.
