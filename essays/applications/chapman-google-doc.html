Dodge College of Film and Media Arts, MFA in Film and Television Producing

Priority deadline: 15 November 2026 (fellowship preference)

1. Statement of Intent

Express your reasons for wanting to join the program. What are your personal and career goals, and how will this particular degree from Chapman University help you reach those goals?

I learned to see on a phone. From 2016 I edited every photograph from my family's trips, first with filters, then with cutouts and new backgrounds, and had every one printed. I now call them contrast frames, made to stand out before I knew what they should say.

Then came a camera, and I worked at it until there was nothing on it I had not tried. In Monaco I taught myself macro, hand-blended HDR and long exposures of a changing sky, and from above I caught an F1 car flat out on the straight, dead centre in the frame. These were balanced frames, built on composition rather than effect.

At the Raghu Rai Center for Photography, Nitin Rai spent months teaching me fashion shoots with a fixed camera. On one assignment he called in a model for a commercial shoot, and I hid in a corner, trying to get better pictures than his Vogue-style studio set-up. I got some beautiful frames, and so did he. That corner is where I first wanted an impact frame, one that holds because of who is in it, not the edit or the composition. Nitin noticed the corner more than the frames: I loved a camera free to move, and what I could make with it, far more than one fixed in place. I finished the course probably way too confident, and sure of one thing: the camera I wanted was the one that moved.

Whistling Woods International trained me as a cinematographer, and its student sets gave me a second kind of work: classmates wanted their shoots photographed and filmed from behind the scenes. Even on days with no set of my own I wanted to be on one, for a beautiful set, people at work and candid moments. By then I was photographing the people who make a film rather than the film itself.

The sets I have worked on since, as a cinematographer and now as a camera assistant, are in my portfolio list. There, what I could put in the frame had mostly been decided earlier, by a schedule, a budget or a location, and nobody holding a camera could change it. I want to work where those decisions are made, though I have yet to work in a production office.

Chapman's MFA teaches that work in sequence: story development, financing, entertainment law, and marketing and distribution. After years of photographing other people's crews, I would be producing with one. The thesis asks producers to make advanced films with Dodge's film production students while developing an industry-ready project, the double practice my studio will need to make one project as it develops the next. The required internship could put me inside a production office at last.

My career goal is a creative studio in India of 8 to 30 people, where talented newcomers without industry contacts are paid and trained. My personal goal is to notice in each of them what Nitin Rai noticed in me. If Chapman teaches me to decide what reaches the frame before anyone rolls, and I can pass that on, the studio will hold for the same reason my best frames do: because of who is in it.

3. Project description essay

Describe a large project or event that you planned where you were required to work within a team. Explain some of the challenges you faced, how you handled them, and how the project ultimately turned out.

The Classroom was my first real video work, and it depended on other people's time as much as on mine. It was a five-part web series that a group of friends and I made for YouTube while we were at Suncity School in Gurugram. We produced it ourselves. Besides co-directing, shooting and editing it, I organised it.

Every episode needed somewhere to shoot, and finding those places fell to me. We were all in school, so exams came first, and I set the shoot days in the gaps between them. A shoot could go ahead only when a place, the cast and a free day lined up, and lining them up, episode after episode, was my job.

The team was our classmates, including the actors and a scriptwriter. They were giving their own time to the series, and five episodes is a long commitment for students with exams of their own. Alongside the scheduling, I kept them involved from one episode to the next, because a series that loses its team halfway through is never finished, however well its first episodes turn out.

Fitting shoots between exams meant that every shoot day mattered, and it was on one of those days that the plan gave way. An actor playing one of the roles dropped out at the last second. Rather than lose the day, we invented a new character on the spot, a funny sports teacher, to take the place of that role, and I had to play him myself.

That left two more problems. The lines had been written for the role we had lost, and our scriptwriter was not with us, so the two of us rewrote them, one on set and one at home, to suit a sports teacher. The new character also needed a costume, and I found a standard tracksuit that cost us nothing. With the sports teacher written in, the story of the episode still held together.

Looking back, that day showed me the half of filmmaking that never appears on screen. Anyone watching the episode sees a funny sports teacher. Nobody sees the actor who dropped out, the lines rewritten between the set and our scriptwriter's home, or the tracksuit that cost nothing. When one arrangement failed, the answer came from the same kind of work that had made the plan: a new role, a rewrite and a costume, found quickly enough that a group of friends did not lose a day they had fitted around their exams.

We completed and released all five episodes, and along the way the series gathered a thousand followers, for whom we made a Diwali special. It was the first project I organised from start to finish, and when it was done I found a confidence I had not had before. The sports-teacher day is still how I picture a producer's job: keeping the day, the people and the story together when a plan gives way, so that all anyone ever sees is the sports teacher.

Dodge College and Argyros College, joint MBA/MFA in Film and Television Producing

Priority deadline: 15 November 2026

1. Statement of Intent (MBA/MFA)

Express your reasons for wanting to join the joint MBA/MFA. Cover personal and career goals and how this particular dual degree from Chapman will help you achieve them.

In 2016 I was a boy obsessed with phones, Huawei's above all, and the pictures on them were finished in the edit. The first skill I taught myself was object selection: cutting a subject out, dropping it onto a background it had never stood in, and highlighting it in an exaggerated, stylish way. Those were my contrast frames, built to seize the eye.

Bored of zooming into pictures on a phone screen, I picked up a camera and taught myself to use it. On a family trip to Monaco I went after the balanced frame: symmetrical landscapes, and seagulls soaring about fifty metres away at the full reach of an 18-135mm.

After The Classroom, my photographs began to move for someone else. I approached a musician at school whose music had blown my mind, and we shot many, many pictures on a secluded hilly road outside the city. The next day, at his request, we made a sequence of stills in which he held steady while the background moved. He loved that too much to risk a new location, so we went back, and this time it was all video: him driving in, opening the back of the car, a graphic title at the top. Each of the three shoots was built on what the last had given us.

The impact frame came when SHLAY, a boutique clothing label a senior from my school had just launched, wanted me for a professional shoot. I no longer wanted the contrast of my editing days or the symmetry of my landscapes; I wanted a frame that meant something about the person I was photographing.

At Whistling Woods International, where I trained in cinematography, student crews made me their behind-the-scenes photographer for stills and video, and more sets asked than I could cover, so I committed to them in advance. The impact frame had found a new subject, the crew, and set after set I watched how a film gets made rather than what it shows. My CV lists the films I have shot and assisted on since.

Now, as a camera assistant, I stand at the end of a long chain of decisions. Whatever was settled in the schedule, the budget, the locations or the casting reaches the camera as something to work around, and I want to be at the start of that chain instead. I have not worked in a production department, and I want to learn the business and the craft side by side.

In Chapman's joint degree, Argyros would teach me to run a company; Dodge would give me the producing courses, from entertainment law to financing, and films made with student directors and writers; and the joint thesis would carry one project through rights, pitching, budgeting, packaging, marketing and distribution.

For myself, I want to read a budget as closely as I read a frame. For my career, after some years in development and production, I plan a studio of 8 to 30 people in India that develops its own films and series and pays its people fairly. If its films carry the impact frame and its books balance as neatly as my landscapes once did, the chain will hold from first decision to last frame.

2. Argyros Statement of Intent

Discuss how Chapman University and the Argyros College will help you achieve your career and personal goals.

Before I studied business, a camera had me asking what a choice costs and what it buys. On trips to Dehradun, where my sibling and two cousins were at school, I shot on a Canon 600D with its kit lens, then traded it for a nifty fifty on a 7D Mark II, a pairing that opened possibilities a better camera could not. Learning how stabilisers work, I asked myself why my camera had none in its body when Sony offered it for less. Economics, business studies and accountancy at Suncity School later gave those questions a vocabulary, but my business training stopped there, and I have never run a company.

The studio I plan in India will face such questions on every project, from what to develop to what each film can afford. At Argyros, beside the Dodge MFA, I would study strategy, to structure a slate; financial management, to manage cash across long projects; and leadership, to build a team and get the best from it. I have practised that last part behind a camera: in my final month at the Raghu Rai Center for Photography, a friend, a complete amateur, modelled for me, and I got her through three outfits in two hours.

Personally, I want to answer those questions for a whole company as readily as I once asked them of a camera, so that its people can trust that the business behind their work is run with the care they bring to the frame.
