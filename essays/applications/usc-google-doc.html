1. Why a producing program

Why do you want to attend a producing program?

As a cinematographer I kept meeting problems on set that had started long before the shoot, often with a cost no one was in charge of, and by the time they reached the camera I could only work around them. I want to learn to solve them where they start.

Photography gave me words for how my eye has changed. The family pictures I once reworked on my phone were contrast frames, built to stand out, and landscapes later taught me balance, but what I want now is an impact frame, one that means something to whoever is in it. At Whistling Woods International the camera turned round. Student sets kept asking me to shoot their behind-the-scenes stills and video, more than I could cover, so I committed to shoots in advance, and on days without a set of my own I still wanted to be on one. Photographing the people making a film rather than the film itself, I watched every department at work.

Most of my jobs have been on camera crews. On Khauf, at Matchbox Studios, I was a camera assistant, and since October 2025 I have held the same role at Bayview Projects under producer Boney Kapoor. On a Finolex commercial for Early Man Productions I was in the direction team, preparing the PPM deck, production sheets and cast references and running costume trials, and at Growth Jockey I planned a shoot for Sleepyhead Mattresses: the equipment, the script, what the film would offer and who it was meant to reach.

I have not yet produced a professional film, developed a script with a writer, raised money for a film or sold one. Those, along with budgets and schedules, are what I want Stark to teach me, in a cohort that studies development, budgeting and finance together, with internships that test the training inside companies. The thesis, a full plan for a project presented to industry professionals, is the test I want to pass before I build a studio in India of 8 to 30 people, where problems are solved before they reach the frame.

2. Unique individual

Tell us about yourself as a unique individual and how your distinctive experiences, identities, values, and/or views of the world have shaped who you are.

When I look at a creative idea, I ask what it needs, what it will cost and who carries the risk, and the habit is older than my film training. At Suncity School in Gurugram my subjects were economics, business studies and accountancy, and no one in my family had a career in film. My parents never quite saw this career coming; work was not something they talked to us about until my last years at school. Still, it grew out of something they already had at home. I edited every photograph from our trips on my phone before it was printed, and today my family's homes are full of my pictures.

Alongside commerce I studied photography at the Raghu Rai Center for Photography, shot press photographs for Suncity MUN, and co-directed, shot, edited and organised a five-part web series with friends. At the time these felt like separate interests: a balance sheet in one class, a frame in another, a shoot to fit between exams. Producing, the work I now want to do, needs all of them at once.

I trained in cinematography and direction at Whistling Woods International, Mumbai, and I have spent most of my working life in the camera department. On my early jobs, as an assistant cinematographer on short films and a camera assistant on Khauf, I was the newest person on set, and I still notice who that is on every set I work on. When I produce, I want the newest person on my crew to be taught the work and given a share of it, and I want the studio I build in India to be a place where that is simply how people start.

3. An insurmountable challenge

Tell us about a time when you faced an insurmountable challenge and the problem solving you employed in response.

When I decided to work in film, I had no clear way in. I was a commerce student in Gurugram, nobody in my family worked in film or photography, and in Indian film many first jobs come through family. If I wanted a place, I would have to earn it.

I split the problem into parts I could work on, starting with skills I could show. I spent about eight months on a fashion and advertising course at the Raghu Rai Center for Photography, where I studied under Nitin Rai, and I became the International Press Photographer for Suncity MUN. I also needed work people could watch, so with friends I made a five-part web series, The Classroom, which we produced ourselves and put on YouTube. I was its co-director, cinematographer and editor, and I did the organising as well: finding places to shoot, fitting shoots in between exams and keeping classmates involved.

For proper training I took a B.Sc. in Filmmaking, Cinematography and Direction at Whistling Woods International, awarded by TISS, which I completed in 2025. The last part was getting people in the industry to trust me, so during the course I took every job within reach: five larger-scale short films as assistant cinematographer, Khauf as a camera assistant under Pankaj Kumar, a place on the direction team of a Finolex commercial, and the planning of a Sleepyhead Mattresses shoot for Growth Jockey.

Today I am a camera assistant on a project under producer Boney Kapoor. The problem is smaller now, and I still handle it the same way, by working out what I am missing and going after it. What I am missing now is development, financing, and the budgeting and scheduling of a professional production. USC's motto gives the palm to whoever earns it, and camera crews taught me to earn a place one job at a time. The next one I want is where a production's decisions are made, and Stark is where I want to learn to make them.

7. Something you are trying to improve

Tell us something about yourself that you are trying to improve and how you are going about it.

I am trying to understand the work that happens before a shoot, and to be exact about how much of it I have only watched. I trained as a cinematographer and work as a camera assistant, so most of what I know about schedules, budgets and development I have watched from the camera department rather than done myself, and I have overestimated what I knew before: when my photography course ended I was, looking back, probably far too confident. 8. An emotionally significant experience

Describe an emotionally significant experience in your life and how it affected you.

During COVID, while I was still in school, I was media manager for Mitti to Matka, an initiative that helped Gurugram's potters after the markets closed. We bought their pots at fair prices and auctioned them, and we took food and sanitary supplies to their families. Until then I had made pictures to stand out. Here buyers saw the pots only through my photographs, so the quality of a picture could change what a family earned that week.

That made me careful, with the photographs and with the money. Alongside the photographs, videos and graphics, I ran the website and the administration, where the arithmetic was plain: every rupee we spent badly was a rupee a family did not receive. Over those months I also started to feel responsible for the families themselves, not only for their pots.

What affected me most was seeing how much we could not do. Selling pots and delivering supplies helped, but it could not make up for what a whole community had lost in the lockdown. That was hard to accept, and we kept doing the part we could do.

I still work by the arithmetic of that year. When the money belongs to someone else, I treat each rupee as one that somebody will go without if I waste it, and I would rather tell people at the start what a project can do for them than let them hope for more. I want to bring that to producing, where the people a film affects include the crew who make it.

9. Favourite existing work

Do you have a favorite existing film, TV show, web series, book, or play? Explain why it's a favorite.

My favourite is The Lunchbox (2013). A lunch delivered to the wrong office in Mumbai starts an exchange of letters between two lonely strangers, and the film never raises its voice. As a viewer, and as a cinematographer, I love its restraint: the sounds of a kitchen, the train commute, a letter read over lunch. As someone who wants to produce, I admire how it was put together. It was an international co-production, launched at a major festival and released around the world, so a very local Mumbai story reached audiences far outside India. That is the kind of film I want my studio to make: a specific Indian story, made with care and financed well enough to find an audience abroad.

1. Cinematic Arts Personal Statement

The committee seeks creativity, self-awareness, and vision. Focus on your unique individuality, distinctive experiences, identities, values, and worldview as a filmmaker. Describe the stories you want to tell.

For most of my training I have stood behind a camera, a good place to learn who on a set keeps things running. I came to it from a commerce classroom in Gurugram, with an eye built one picture at a time: first contrast frames, phone edits chasing attention, then balanced frames, where composition did the work, and now impact frames, about the people inside them.

Studying at the Raghu Rai Center for Photography, with Nitin Rai among my teachers, showed me that I wanted a camera free to move. With friends at Suncity School I made The Classroom, a five-part YouTube series I co-directed, shot, edited and organised around our exams. Photographing a musician from school on a hilly road behind the city, I moved from stills to a sequence in which he held still while the background moved, then to a shoot that was all video.

During my B.Sc. at Whistling Woods International, in filmmaking, cinematography and direction, completed in 2025 and awarded by TISS, I was assistant cinematographer on five larger-scale short films and lead cinematographer on independent films. Student crews kept me busy as their behind-the-scenes photographer, booked ahead because I could not reach every set. I worked as a camera assistant under Pankaj Kumar on Khauf, for Matchbox Studios, and have been one at Bayview Projects under producer Boney Kapoor since October 2025. On a Finolex commercial I joined the direction team, preparing the production sheets, cast references and PPM deck, and planned a Sleepyhead Mattresses shoot for Growth Jockey. Behind the camera or photographing the crew, I found that the people keeping a shoot going were rarely on screen.

I want to make films about people like them, who keep things working inside rules they did not write: an ISRO technician in 1975 Orissa keeping forty village televisions alive, until Emergency officials turn the evening viewings into round-ups; a Balti homestay hustler who has to help his grandmother marry two glaciers, one on each side of the Line of Control; an incorruptible prohibition constable at a Bihar border check post who has to get one quarter-bottle of liquor across. They are often funny, and about work, family and the systems people live inside.

On crews I saw how much of each day was settled by the schedule, budget, locations and cast before anything reached my frame, and I want to make those decisions. I have not worked in a production department or taken a project from idea to financing; that is what I want to learn. At SCA I would train in every crew role first, then concentrate on producing, alongside classmates I expect to keep working with. After a few years in development and production, I plan to set up a studio in India, with 8 to 30 people, that makes films like these, and if those films put the people who hold things together at the centre of the frame, SCA will have taught me what I came for.

Karmanasa bridge, Bihar-Uttar Pradesh border, 5:40 a.m. Liquor shops line the Uttar Pradesh bank. A kilometre of trucks idles on the Grand Trunk Road, waiting to enter Bihar, dry since 2016. Below runs the Karmanasa, the river nobody touches. A boat of pilgrims crosses it with their feet drawn up off the wet boards.

At the barrier, BHOLA (52), prohibition constable, hands back a driver's licence with the hundred-rupee note still inside. RANI, his black Labrador, sits beside a truck of onions, chest out. A constable digs. Under the onions: whisky. On a check-post shelf labelled MUSEUM, old seizures: a hollowed coconut, a fake pregnancy belly, a sealed can of Ganga water. On the duty board, someone has chalked a halo over his name.

At home that night, his FATHER (84), a retired GT Road trucker, lies beside an oxygen cylinder. He points at a photo: himself at thirty, a quarter-bottle of rum raised, one foot on a truck bumper painted RANI. Then he lays two fingers against his steel tumbler.

Next morning Bhola buys a quarter on the Uttar Pradesh bank, funnels it into the museum's coconut and walks back. Rani sits at his feet, chest out. The constables lower their tea. Bhola throws her ball and chalks TRAINING DRILL beside the halo.

At dusk he tries again, seven months pregnant. Rani abandons a half-searched truck to sit at his feet. Phones come up along the queue. He retreats, one hand on his bump.

Next day he joins the pilgrims crossing into Bihar, the Ganga water can on his shoulder like theirs. Rani sits at his feet, tail sweeping the road. Fifty pilgrims step back from him as if he were the river.

Night, a kilometre downstream. Bhola takes off his boots and socks and lifts the quarter over his head. His big toe hovers above the black water. And hovers. He puts his socks back on.

Morning. Bhola wheels his father and his oxygen cylinder through his own barrier and across the bridge. Outside the first liquor shop he unclips Rani's working harness and sits in full uniform with a glass of tea. His father pours two fingers of rum into his steel tumbler and raises it to Bihar. Bhola raises his tea.

Rani sniffs the tumbler, looks at Bhola and lies down across both their feet. Over the water, small, the barrier lifts for the next truck.

4. Creative team question

Describe a difficult situation that you have faced with other people in your creative life and how you handled it.

The Classroom was a five-part web series my friends and I made at Suncity School for YouTube, and I did two kinds of work on it. I co-directed, shot and edited it, which kept me behind the frame, and I organised it, which meant fitting every shoot day around our exams and keeping my classmates involved.

On one shoot day an actor pulled out at the very last moment. We did not want to lose the day, so we made up a new character there and then, a funny sports teacher, and I took the part myself, which put me inside the frame as well as behind it.

That turned a casting problem into a writing problem as well. Our scriptwriter was at home, and the lines we had were written for a character who was no longer in the episode. The two of us worked on them remotely and rewrote them for the sports teacher, so the shoot could go ahead with words that fitted the person now in the scene. The new character also had to look the part, and I found him a standard tracksuit, which cost us nothing.

We kept the shoot day, and the episode's story still held together. We finished all five episodes and released them on YouTube.

Looking back, the difficulty was with people, and so was the answer: an actor who pulled out, a writer working from home, and a group of friends with a day we had already fitted around exams. What I took from it is that organising a film, finding the time, the people and the next workable choice, is creative work in its own right, and that a plan can change at the last moment and still hold if everyone can see where they fit in the new one.

That is the work I want to learn properly as a producer. As a camera assistant on professional sets I have seen how much of a shoot day depends on decisions made before it starts, and I want to be the one making them early, so that a crew, like my classmates that day, can keep its day and its story.

Title: SUN PATCH

Logline: On a rooftop in a Gurugram urban village, a student cramming for tomorrow's exam chases a shrinking patch of sunlight as a glass tower's shadow swallows his roof.

Shot plan (phone on a tripod, one rooftop facing the towers, shot across one afternoon):

1. Autobiographical character sketch

Write a creative essay or short story that evokes an understanding of how you became who you are.

The Ledger and the Frame

In the accountancy class at Suncity School in Gurugram, every rupee has to land somewhere, debit on one side and credit on the other, with nothing left over. The boy who studies commerce there comes from a family with nobody in film or photography, though his eye began years earlier, on a phone, where he added cutouts, new backgrounds and exaggerated focus to family photographs to make them catch attention. He will later call these contrast frames: pictures made to stand out more than to say anything.

A camera follows the phone, and landscapes on trips to Dehradun and Monaco teach him the balanced frame, in which everything sits where it should. Light, he finds, follows rules as strict as a balance sheet's.

At the Raghu Rai Center for Photography, during a commercial shoot with a model, he hides in a corner of the studio, looking for better frames than the set-up was built to give. He comes away with some beautiful frames, and so does his teacher. Over the eight-month course he comes to want an impact frame, a meaningful frame rather than a contrast frame.

Meanwhile, at school, the camera starts to move. He and his friends make a web series, The Classroom, which he co-directs, shoots and edits. They have no producer, so he does that work too, though nobody calls it producing: he finds somewhere to shoot each episode, fits the shoot days between exams and keeps his classmates coming back until five episodes are up on YouTube. When COVID closes the markets in 2020, he becomes media manager of Mitti to Matka, which buys Gurugram potters' pots at fair prices and auctions them, and he photographs the pots and keeps the administration in order. For the first time the ledger and the frame are one job, and other people's income depends on it.

In Mumbai he trains as a cinematographer and director at Whistling Woods International, finishing his TISS-awarded B.Sc. in 2025. He assists the cinematographer on five larger-scale short films and works as a camera assistant on Khauf, at Matchbox Studios, under Pankaj Kumar. For Early Man Productions he joins the direction team of a Finolex commercial, preparing cast references, production sheets and the PPM deck and running costume trials, and for Growth Jockey he plans a Sleepyhead Mattresses shoot: its equipment, script, offer and audience. Now a camera assistant at Bayview Projects under producer Boney Kapoor, he keeps seeing that a shoot day arrives on set already shaped by choices made somewhere else.

He has not yet worked where those decisions are made, and the part he most wants to learn comes earlier still: the script, while the story is being written and the budget and the audience are open questions. Every frame on a set, he has come to see, was a sentence first.

He plans to build a studio in India, with a team of 8 to 30, that develops its own films and series, and he wants to begin where its frames begin, with the writing. If its stories can be written with their cost and their audience counted from the first draft, the ledger and the frame will start on the same page.

2. Creative challenge, Scene A

Two very different kinds of people stuck in an elevator on New Year's Eve.

Title: TAKE IN

INT. GOODS LIFT, KAILASH GUPTA & SONS CLOTH STORE, SHAHJAHANPUR, UTTAR PRADESH - NIGHT

An iron cage the width of a wardrobe. Above the lever, a brass plate polished to gold: KAILASH GUPTA & SONS. LIFT NUMBER ONE. 1962. GOODS ONLY.

Through the mesh, New Year's rockets burst green over Chowk. In the lane, a cane truck grinds past.

BASHIR AHMAD (71), tailor. Woollen cap, half-moon spectacles, a yellow tape round his neck, chalk in his breast pocket. Over one forearm, a black wool suit in a dry-cleaner's sheet.

Beside him, SUKHDEV VERMA (49), cane commission agent. Camel overcoat, two gold rings, tinted glasses at night. A ten-day beard where a famous moustache used to be.

SUKHDEV

(in Hindi, a borrowed Lucknow softness)

You do understand? I am the cousin. From Lucknow.

BASHIR

So you said at the door. For the suit and the old measurements.

SUKHDEV

For the family's records. Usha bhabhi wants every number.

BASHIR

The registers are upstairs.

He pulls the lever. The cage groans and climbs.

BASHIR (CONT'D)

Seth Gupta says there is no other lift between Bareilly and Lucknow.

The cage jolts and stops between floors. The bulb dies. The lane goes dark under the rockets.

SUKHDEV

Overloaded. Everybody has fairy lights tonight and one wire.

His phone torch snaps on.

BASHIR

Hold the light up, cousin. Level with your ear.

SUKHDEV

Why?

BASHIR

So I can see. Stand straight.

Sukhdev lifts the light and rises onto the balls of his feet.

BASHIR (CONT'D)

Heels down. Shoulders down. Left arm out.

SUKHDEV

I am the cousin. Cousins are not measured.

BASHIR

Everybody is measured by somebody. Arm.

Sukhdev edges toward the gate instead. Bashir reads him anyway, one palm on the point of the shoulder.

BASHIR (CONT'D)

Chest, forty. Sleeve, twenty-five. Left shoulder low. A scooter, every day, to the cane centres.

SUKHDEV

All of us Vermas have that shoulder.

BASHIR

Then I am sorry for your loss. Sukhdev was a good customer. Half in advance, every time.

SUKHDEV

That was Sukhdev. Always the advance.

BASHIR

In November he ordered this suit. Make it for my funeral, he said, so the mourners see I had taste.

SUKHDEV

4. Most challenging moment

Convey in writing the greatest challenge you have faced and how you responded to it.

In 2020, before I had finished school, COVID closed Gurugram's markets, and the city's potters were left with pots ready to sell and nowhere to sell them. I joined Mitti to Matka, a volunteer initiative set up to help them, as its media manager, and it handed me the hardest problem I have faced: how do you sell a clay pot to someone who cannot pick it up and turn it over?

A market, I came to see, is several things at once. It is a place where the pots stand, a buyer who can hold one, a price both sides accept, and someone keeping count of the money. The lockdown had taken every one of them away at the same moment, and our work was to put each back without a single stall.

We began with the price, because the families could not wait for a sale. We bought the pots from them ourselves, at fair prices, so they were paid before anything had been sold. The buyers came through auctions, and most of my part lay there, in building a stall that nobody could walk up to. A photograph had to do what a buyer's hands could not. I photographed and filmed the pots, made the graphics, ran the website that took the stall's place, and kept the administration in order, which is where the counting was done. Alongside this, the team worked with the potters on improving their designs, and spent what it could spare on food and sanitary supplies for their families.

None of it could be done once and left. We kept the auctions and the deliveries going, and I stayed on as media manager into 2021. The administration I kept in order was no longer a classroom exercise, because the potters' families were relying on it.

That year showed me that a project is a set of parts that have to work together, and that losing one can stop all the rest. The studio I mean to start in India will be small, between 8 and 30 people making their own films and series, and I want it run with the habits Mitti to Matka forced on us: each part of a project, from the script to its audience, worked out before anyone depends on it, and the money counted in the open. If the people who make its films can rely on that from the first draft, Mitti to Matka will have taught me the most useful thing I know.
