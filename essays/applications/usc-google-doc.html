1. Why a producing program

Why do you want to attend a producing program?

My eye has never stayed still for long. In stills it went from contrast frames, built to stand out, to balanced frames, to frames with impact; then it wanted to move, and in the end it turned round to face the people behind the camera.

At the Raghu Rai Center for Photography, when Nitin Rai called in a model for a commercial shoot, I hid in a corner trying to get better pictures than the Vogue-style studio set-up. I got some beautiful frames, and so did he. Looking back, my corner only existed because he had brought in the model and built the set-up I was trying to beat, and that is the work I now want to do.

The moving camera took me to Whistling Woods International to train as a cinematographer, and there it turned round. Student sets brought me in for their behind-the-scenes stills and video, and on days without a set of my own I still wanted to be on one. Photographing the people making a film rather than the film, I watched every department at once, and a set seen whole stops being a shot list and becomes a day that someone had to arrange.

It was only as a camera assistant that I saw where the problems reaching the camera begin: long before the shoot, often with a cost nobody was in charge of. By the time they arrive I can only work around them, and I want to learn to solve them where they start. I have not yet taken a script through development, raised money for a film or sold one. I want to learn all three in a cohort that studies development, budgeting and finance together, with internships that test it inside companies. The thesis, a full plan for one project presented to industry professionals, is the test I want to pass before I build a small studio in India. My eye has gone from the frame to the set to the people who arrange it, and producing is where it wants to stay, deciding what reaches the frame.

2. Unique individual

Tell us about yourself as a unique individual and how your distinctive experiences, identities, values, and/or views of the world have shaped who you are.

In 2016, like many boys my age, I was obsessed with phones and their cameras, Huawei's above all, and before I owned a camera I had become my family's editor. Every photograph from our trips went through my phone, every one was pushed until it stood out, and every one was printed. Those were my contrast frames.

Boredom moved me on. Only when zooming into pictures on a screen stopped holding my interest did I pick up a camera, and after my failed stint with Filmora, a video editor, it felt like a new achievement. That camera was a Canon 600D with its kit lens, carried on trips to Dehradun, where my sibling and two cousins were at school, and I taught myself everything on it until there was nothing I had not tried. Then I traded it in for a nifty fifty, the cheap 50mm, on a 7D Mark II, a body whose autofocus was built for sport and wildlife, and that pairing opened possibilities beyond what a better camera could offer. With it I used manual focus creatively for the first time, played with bokeh and learned how stabilisers work, and I began asking why my camera had no in-body stabilisation when Sony offered it for less.

That was a commerce student's question, and at Suncity School in Gurugram I became one, studying economics, business studies and accountancy, and learning to read a balance sheet in the same years I was learning to read light. Between them, the camera and the classroom taught me that the right combination can do more than the expensive one, and that someone has to check the numbers. I still meet every idea that way, asking what it needs, what it will cost and who carries the risk, and that habit, more than any single credit, is what I would bring to a producing cohort.

3. An insurmountable challenge

Tell us about a time when you faced an insurmountable challenge and the problem solving you employed in response.

The closest I have come to a problem with no way round it arrived on a shoot day of The Classroom, a five-part series for YouTube that a group of us made while still at Suncity School. It was my first real video work, and we produced it ourselves. I co-directed, shot and edited it, and I also kept it organised, which meant fitting every shoot day around our exams.

That day, one of our actors dropped out at the last second. There is no fixing an actor who is not coming, so instead of trying to fix that we changed what the scene needed. We invented a new character on the spot, a funny sports teacher, to stand in for the missing role, and the part fell to me, so I stepped out from behind my own camera to play him.

Inventing him solved one problem and created two. Each of his lines had been written for the character we had lost, and our scriptwriter was at home rather than on set, so we reworked the dialogue together at a distance until it sounded like a sports teacher. He needed clothes as well, and I got hold of a standard tracksuit without spending anything. Nothing was postponed, the plot survived the swap, and the series went on to its fifth episode.

Looking back, what solved it was separating what could not move from what could. The day stayed fixed. The character, his words and his clothes did not, so those were what we changed, and we changed the words with the writer rather than around him. As a producer I want to work the same way on a larger scale: when a plan breaks, hold on to what matters, rebuild the rest quickly, and do it with the people who will have to make it work.

8. An emotionally significant experience

Describe an emotionally significant experience in your life and how it affected you.

The day before I left for Europe, I shot for SHLAY, a senior's new boutique clothing brand, with a model who had graduated from my school before I joined middle school. For a long time I had believed my camera was doing the work, until I put it in someone else's hands and saw that what I knew mattered more than what I held. It was a shoot well done, and it was work.

France was something else. I could not stop using the camera, and not because of the architecture, and not because of the camera. I ran on autopilot: motion long exposures, frozen water, birds, strangers, and my own family, whom I photographed as if on black-and-white film. I had built my colour profiles before travelling, so I never once felt the need to edit a frame. Between that shoot and that trip I understood the difference between work done well and work I could not stop doing.

My family never knew this would become my career; work was not something my parents talked to us about until my senior years at school. Today their houses are full of my polished prints. I sometimes miss the amateur auto-exposure moments when everyone was made to smile, but I prefer candids now, because now the images bring smiles.

That is the change I care about most. I used to want my pictures to be looked at; now I want them to give something back to the people in them. France showed me how to get there: do the deciding before the trip, and the moment itself can be left alone. I want to produce in that order, with the decisions made early, so that on the day the people in front of the camera and behind it can simply be themselves. A set prepared like that is a candid, and nobody on it has to be made to smile.

9. Favourite existing work

Do you have a favorite existing film, TV show, web series, book, or play? Explain why it's a favorite.

My favourite is The Lunchbox, released in 2013. A lunch delivered to the wrong office in Mumbai starts an exchange of letters between two lonely strangers, and the film never raises its voice. As a viewer, and as a cinematographer, I love its restraint: the sounds of a kitchen, the train commute, a letter read over lunch. As someone who wants to produce, I admire how it was put together. It was an international co-production, launched at a major festival and released around the world, so a very local Mumbai story reached audiences far outside India. That is the kind of film I want my studio to make: a specific Indian story, made with care and financed well enough to find an audience abroad.

1. Cinematic Arts Personal Statement

The committee seeks creativity, self-awareness, and vision. Focus on your unique individuality, distinctive experiences, identities, values, and worldview as a filmmaker. Describe the stories you want to tell.

The people I most want to film keep things running off screen. It took me three kinds of frame to learn to see them.

It began on a phone in 2016, with holiday pictures I edited until they were louder than the moments they came from. These were contrast frames, made to be noticed.

A camera slowed me down. In Monaco I left the shutter open on skies that would not keep still and, from above, framed an F1 car flat out down the straight, exactly mid-frame: balanced frames, where composition did the work.

In eleventh grade a schoolmate's music floored me, and I asked to photograph him on a quiet hill road outside the city. He loved the many, many pictures, and the next day he wanted a run of stills where he stays put as the world behind him slides away. He loved that so much he would not gamble on a new location, so we went back and made it all video: him driving in, opening the back of the car, a graphic title across the top. It was where my photography and my video met in work for someone else, and from the second day the plan was his.

By the time SHLAY, a boutique clothing label, asked me for a professional shoot, I was carrying a Nikon Z6 and a weighty 24-70 and wanted more than contrast or balance: an impact frame, one that said something.

Whistling Woods International gave that wish its subject, though not the one I enrolled for. I went there to become a cinematographer, and student crews kept asking me instead to photograph and film their own sets, more of them than I could reach, so I booked them in advance. There is no film in those pictures, only the making of one: beautiful sets, people at their jobs, candid moments, and faces the finished films never show. Those were the frames I kept going back for.

I want to film people like them, working inside rules they did not write: an ISRO technician keeping forty village televisions alive in 1975 Orissa until Emergency officials turn the viewings into round-ups; a Balti homestay hustler helping his grandmother marry two glaciers across the Line of Control; an incorruptible Bihar prohibition constable who has to get one quarter-bottle past his own check post. They are often funny.

On the crews in my portfolio list, the schedule, budget, locations and cast had settled most of each day before anything reached my frame. Those are the decisions I mean to make, and since I have yet to work in a production department, I want SCA to teach me how. Then I plan to build a studio of 8 to 30 people in India that makes films like these, and if its films put the people who hold things together at the centre of the frame, I will have my impact frame: the ones who keep things running, on screen at last.

Karmanasa bridge, Bihar-Uttar Pradesh border, 5:40 a.m. Liquor shops line the Uttar Pradesh bank. A kilometre of trucks idles on the Grand Trunk Road, waiting to enter Bihar, dry since 2016. Below runs the Karmanasa, the river nobody touches. A boat of pilgrims crosses it with their feet drawn up off the wet boards.

At the barrier, BHOLA (52), prohibition constable, hands back a driver's licence with the hundred-rupee note still inside. RANI, his black Labrador, sits beside a truck of onions, chest out. A constable digs. Under the onions: whisky. On a check-post shelf labelled MUSEUM, old seizures: a hollowed coconut, a fake pregnancy belly, a sealed can of Ganga water. On the duty board, someone has chalked a halo over his name.

At home that night, his FATHER (84), a retired GT Road trucker, lies beside an oxygen cylinder. He points at a photo: himself at thirty, a quarter-bottle of rum raised, one foot on a truck bumper painted RANI. Then he lays two fingers against his steel tumbler.

Next morning Bhola buys a quarter on the Uttar Pradesh bank, funnels it into the museum's coconut and walks back. Rani sits at his feet, chest out. The constables lower their tea. Bhola throws her ball and chalks TRAINING DRILL beside the halo.

At dusk he tries again, seven months pregnant. Rani abandons a half-searched truck to sit at his feet. Phones come up along the queue. He retreats, one hand on his bump.

Next day he joins the pilgrims crossing into Bihar, the Ganga water can on his shoulder like theirs. Rani sits at his feet, tail sweeping the road. Fifty pilgrims step back from him as if he were the river.

Night, a kilometre downstream. Bhola takes off his boots and socks and lifts the quarter over his head. His big toe hovers above the black water. And hovers. He puts his socks back on.

Morning. Bhola wheels his father and his oxygen cylinder through his own barrier and across the bridge. Outside the first liquor shop he unclips Rani's working harness and sits in full uniform with a glass of tea. His father pours two fingers of rum into his steel tumbler and raises it to Bihar. Bhola raises his tea.

Rani sniffs the tumbler, looks at Bhola and lies down across both their feet. Over the water, small, the barrier lifts for the next truck.

4. Creative team question

Describe a difficult situation that you have faced with other people in your creative life and how you handled it.

Our actor withdrew just as we were about to shoot, on a day my friends and I had already squeezed between exams. We were at Suncity School making The Classroom, a five-part web series for YouTube and my first serious work in video. I co-directed and shot it, and I was also the one who kept it going: the day had been found in the exam timetable, and everyone who had turned up was a classmate I had kept on board.

Cancelling would have cost us the day, so we wrote a comic sports teacher into the episode there and then, and I took the part, which put the cameraman in front of the lens.

That turned a casting problem into a writing problem. Every line the new character would speak had been meant for the role that had just disappeared, and the person who wrote them was working from home, so the two of us rewrote them, one on set and one off it, until they suited a sports teacher and fitted the person now in the scene. He also needed a costume, and I found him a plain tracksuit that cost us nothing.

We kept the shoot day, and the story of the episode still held together. All five episodes got finished, and I came out of the series with a confidence I had not had before.

The trouble came from people, and so did the way out: an actor who pulled out, a writer who could only help from home, and a group of friends whose shoot day, planned around exams, was suddenly at risk. What I took from it is that organising a film, finding the time, the people and the next workable choice, is creative work in its own right, and that a plan can change at the last moment and still hold if everyone can see where they fit in the new one. That is the part of filmmaking I want to be answerable for, and on a professional set it is called producing.

1. Autobiographical character sketch

Write a creative essay or short story that evokes an understanding of how you became who you are.

Working Backwards

Most photographers begin with a camera. This one begins at the edit. In 2016, on a phone, he works over his family's travel pictures, first with filters, then with graphics. The first technique he works out alone is selection: trace the subject, lift it from its world, drop it into another and make its focus so emphatic it borders on theatre. Later he will call these contrast frames, built so the eye has nowhere else to go.

From the edit he steps back to the camera. In Monaco, travelling with his family, he runs an 18-135mm out to the end of its zoom and holds gulls soaring some fifty metres away. These are balanced frames, weighed in the viewfinder rather than fixed afterwards.

His last month at the Raghu Rai Center for Photography brings a shoot of his own making. He asks a friend to model, a complete amateur, and between them they get through three outfits in two hours. Now the picture depends less on what he can do with the camera than on what he can draw out of the person in front of it. A third kind of frame, the impact frame, a picture that hits with meaning rather than volume, is still ahead of him, and he will look for it through a lens for years before he learns where it is made.

The frames start to move with The Classroom, a five-part web series he and his school friends make for YouTube, and with them come two problems a still never had. One is a story that must hold from one episode to the next. The other is people: classmates who need somewhere to film, dates that examinations leave free, and reasons to keep turning up for five episodes. The camera is his, and so, it turns out, is the organising.

Whistling Woods International trains him as a cinematographer, but its student filmmakers want him for something else: photographs and footage from behind the scenes. With no shot of its own to serve, his camera roams, and what it finds is the beauty of a well-built set, people lost in their work and moments nobody posed. Turned from the scene to the people behind it, he starts to see how much of a film is settled before anyone calls action.

On the professional sets that follow, and now as a camera assistant, he keeps finding that the trouble landing on the camera team began somewhere upstream, in choices made long before anyone unpacked a lens. Those are a producer's decisions, not yet his, and the ones he most wants to learn come first, on the page, while the story, its cost and its audience are still open. Every frame on a set, he has come to see, was a sentence first.

His plan is a studio in India with a team of 8 to 30, developing its own films and series, and he wants to begin where its frames begin, with the writing. If its scripts weigh their cost and audience from the first draft, fewer of its problems will ever reach a camera, and ten years of working backwards will have ended where a film begins.

2. Creative challenge, Scene A

Two very different kinds of people stuck in an elevator on New Year's Eve.

Title: TAKE IN

INT. GOODS LIFT, KAILASH GUPTA & SONS CLOTH STORE, SHAHJAHANPUR, UTTAR PRADESH - NIGHT

An iron cage the width of a wardrobe. Above the lever, a brass plate polished to gold: KAILASH GUPTA & SONS. LIFT NUMBER ONE. 1962. GOODS ONLY.

Through the mesh, New Year's rockets burst green over Chowk. In the lane, a cane truck grinds past.

BASHIR AHMAD (71), tailor. Woollen cap, half-moon spectacles, a yellow tape round his neck, chalk in his breast pocket. Over one forearm, a black wool suit in a dry-cleaner's sheet.

Beside him, SUKHDEV VERMA (49), cane commission agent. Camel overcoat, two gold rings, tinted glasses at night. A ten-day beard where a famous moustache used to be.

SUKHDEV

(in Hindi, a borrowed Lucknow softness)

You do understand? I am the cousin. From Lucknow.

BASHIR

So you said at the door. For the suit and the old measurements.

SUKHDEV

For the family's records. Usha bhabhi wants every number.

BASHIR

The registers are upstairs.

He pulls the lever. The cage groans and climbs.

BASHIR (CONT'D)

Seth Gupta says there is no other lift between Bareilly and Lucknow.

The cage jolts and stops between floors. The bulb dies. The lane goes dark under the rockets.

SUKHDEV

Overloaded. Everybody has fairy lights tonight and one wire.

His phone torch snaps on.

BASHIR

Hold the light up, cousin. Level with your ear.

SUKHDEV

Why?

BASHIR

So I can see. Stand straight.

Sukhdev lifts the light and rises onto the balls of his feet.

BASHIR (CONT'D)

Heels down. Shoulders down. Left arm out.

SUKHDEV

I am the cousin. Cousins are not measured.

BASHIR

Everybody is measured by somebody. Arm.

Sukhdev edges toward the gate instead. Bashir reads him anyway, one palm on the point of the shoulder.

BASHIR (CONT'D)

Chest, forty. Sleeve, twenty-five. Left shoulder low. A scooter, every day, to the cane centres.

SUKHDEV

All of us Vermas have that shoulder.

BASHIR

Then I am sorry for your loss. Sukhdev was a good customer. Half in advance, every time.

SUKHDEV

That was Sukhdev. Always the advance.

BASHIR

In November he ordered this suit. Make it for my funeral, he said, so the mourners see I had taste.

SUKHDEV

(too loudly)

Sounds like him.

BASHIR

Yesterday the police released the body. Usha's brother came to my shop crying, with this.

4. Most challenging moment

Convey in writing the greatest challenge you have faced and how you responded to it.

In 2020, before I had finished school, COVID closed Gurugram's markets, and the city's potters were left with pots ready to sell and nowhere to sell them. I joined Mitti to Matka, an initiative set up to help them, as its media manager, and it handed me the hardest problem I have faced: how do you sell a clay pot to someone who cannot pick it up and turn it over?

A market, I came to see, is several things at once. It is a place where the pots stand, a buyer who can hold one, a price both sides accept, and someone keeping count of the money. The lockdown had taken every one of them away at the same moment, and our work was to put each back without a single stall.

We began with the price, because the families could not wait for a sale. We bought the pots from them ourselves, at fair prices, so they were paid before anything had been sold. The buyers came through auctions, and most of my part lay there, in building a stall that nobody could walk up to. The stall became a website, which I ran. Its shelves became my photographs, films and graphics of the pots, and they had to do what a buyer's hands could not. The counting became the administration I kept. Whatever the team could spare went on sanitary supplies and food for the potters' families.

None of it could be done once and left. We kept the auctions and the deliveries going into 2021, and the money and paperwork I kept in order stopped being an exercise from my accountancy class, because the potters' families were relying on them.

That year showed me that a project is a set of parts that have to work together, and that losing one can stop all the rest. It also showed me what a picture is for when someone else's living depends on it: letting a stranger understand something he cannot touch, well enough to pay for it. A screenplay is sold the same way: everyone who decides whether it is made reads it before any of it exists. That is how I want to write, for the reader who cannot pick the thing up and turn it over.
