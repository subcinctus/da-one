1. Tisch Statement of Purpose

Who you are and why you're applying. In what ways do you plan to contribute to the program and beyond? What are your academic and professional goals? How have your experiences, successes, and failures shaped how you see the world? What kinds of stories do you hope to tell?

The first images I worked on were photographs from our family trips: I edited every one on my phone, cutting out subjects and blending in new backgrounds in an exaggerated, stylish way, and had them all printed. I now call those contrast frames. Trips with a camera taught me the balanced frame, and people taught me the impact frame, which matters to whoever is in it. Nobody in my family works in film, yet I trained as a cinematographer and work as a camera assistant. I am applying to Tisch Graduate Film and the Stern dual degree to become a producer who takes a story from idea to audience.

At Suncity School in Gurugram I studied economics, business studies and accountancy and was the International Press Photographer for Suncity MUN. As a student at the Raghu Rai Center for Photography, I once hid in a corner during a commercial shoot with a model, looking for my own frames instead of the studio set-up, and my teacher saw that I loved a camera free to move more than a fixed one. I came away with good frames, and so did he.

With friends I made The Classroom, a five-part web series we produced ourselves for YouTube, and my frame began to move. I co-directed, shot, edited and organised it, finding our locations, fitting shoots around exams and keeping classmates involved.

On one shoot day an actor dropped out at the last second. We invented a new character on the spot, a funny sports teacher, and I played him. Our scriptwriter was at home, so we rewrote the lines together, remotely, and I found a standard tracksuit that cost us nothing. The story of the episode still held together.

At Whistling Woods International in Mumbai I trained in cinematography and direction, completing my B.Sc. in 2025. I was assistant cinematographer on five short films, lead or assistant on independent films, and a camera assistant on Khauf at Matchbox Studios under Pankaj Kumar. Student crews wanted me as their behind-the-scenes photographer, stills and video, on more sets than I could cover, so I committed to shoots in advance, and my frame turned towards the people making a film.

NYU's motto asks students to persevere and to excel. In my case the first came well before the second. On set after set, problems reaching the camera started earlier, in the schedule, budget, locations or cast, and the camera team could only work around them. A producer decides what reaches the frame before anyone rolls, and that turned me towards producing.

On the direction team for a Finolex commercial by Early Man Productions, I prepared production sheets, cast references and the PPM deck and ran costume trials, and for Growth Jockey I planned the equipment, script, offer and audience of a Sleepyhead Mattresses shoot. Since October 2025 I have been a camera assistant at Bayview Projects under producer Boney Kapoor. I have not yet worked in a production office or taken a story through development and financing.

During COVID, still at school, I became media manager for Mitti to Matka, a Gurugram initiative that bought and auctioned struggling potters' work at fair prices and gave their families food and sanitary supplies. I made its photographs, videos and graphics, ran the website and handled administration. A picture could decide a family's income for the week, and I still ask what a story can and cannot do for the people it touches.

The pieces in this application show the films I want to produce: a father's apology without the word sorry in a Rohtak photo studio, a farmer's umbrella over a rain gauge in drought-hit Beed, and an army mechanic keeping India's first Antarctic station warm in 1984 while the others hide the Delhi massacre of Sikhs from him. Each follows people at work when something larger reaches them, which is the impact frame in a story, and each belongs to its place while aiming for audiences in India and the United States.

At Tisch I would bring a cinematographer's eye and experience of Indian sets to an international cohort, and in Introduction to Producing I would produce a classmate's film. Shrihari Sathe, Tisch's head of producing, is an Indian-born independent producer whose work has travelled, and that is the path I want to take.

Academically, I want to learn how films are developed, budgeted, scheduled, financed, distributed and marketed, alongside directing and writing, with Stern adding finance, strategy and management. After a few years in development and production, I plan to start a studio in India of 8 to 30 people making its own films and series. If its crews reach the set with problems already solved and time to look for the impact frame, I will have used Tisch well.

2. Dramatic Story

A two-page story using one theme: (a) two characters, one cannot hear the other; (b) survival depends on an unlikely skill; (c) an intruder saves a delicate situation; (d) your older sibling wakes you in the middle of the night and suggests something marginally illegal; (e) at exactly the wrong moment, a character says out loud what they were thinking; (f) a mother makes amends with her child through humor; (g) an apology is made without ever saying sorry.

Bharat Photo Studio in Rohtak still has the painted backdrop of Switzerland, faded to a pale green lake under pale green mountains. Amit has not stood in front of it for nine years.

He comes in at seven in the evening, when the street is loud with scooters and frying. He has a baby on his shoulder, a passport appointment at ten the next morning and a flight to Toronto in five weeks. Every studio in Delhi he called said three days. His father's studio has always said one hour.

Harish is sixty-eight and doesn't get up from behind the counter. He looks at the baby, then at the clock.

"Name?"

"Zoya."

Harish writes it on an envelope in the slow capitals he uses for customers. He doesn't ask for the surname. Nine years ago he said he would not come to the wedding, and he didn't, and Farzana's family hired a man from Panipat who cut off the tops of everyone's heads.

He pulls the white roll down over Switzerland. He seats Amit on the stool with the baby on his knees and drapes a white towel over Amit's hands so only she shows. He sets the lights the way he always has, the left one higher, the umbrella tilted. Then he crouches, which Amit has never seen him do, so the camera is at the baby's height.

Zoya won't open her eyes. Harish makes the sound he used to make on school photo days, a click of the tongue and a short whistle. Her eyes open. He shoots. He shoots again.

"One is enough," Amit says.

"For the passport."

Amit waits on the bench while the printer warms up. He did his homework on this bench as a boy, under other people's weddings framed along the walls. The walls haven't changed. Brides from 1987 and 1994. A police inspector with a moustache. His own Class 4 school photo, still in the corner.

Harish comes back with the prints, eight small faces on a glossy sheet, and a second envelope, older, soft at the corners. He puts both on the counter and goes back to switch off the lights.

Inside the old envelope are twelve photographs of a garden in Delhi on a winter night. Amit knows the garden. He knows the marigold arch, the cheap tent, the DJ. Every frame is taken from far behind the crowd on a long lens, between strangers' shoulders, past a pillar, through a gap in the tent wall. In one, Farzana is laughing at something Amit has said. In another, the two of them are tiny at the end of an aisle of plastic chairs. The light is good. The focus is perfect. They are printed on the studio's best paper, and they have never been taken out.

At the back of the shop, Harish is coiling cables and doesn't turn around.

Amit lays the baby on the counter, on the soft envelope. She grabs a photograph and holds on.

"How much?" Amit asks, about the passport prints.

"Sit," his father says. "I'll take a bigger one. For the wall."

He rolls the white paper up, and behind them, Switzerland comes back.

3. Short Scene

A cinematic scene between two people that reveals something about the characters. One-sentence description of each character allowed; essential details only; no backstory but incorporate behavior; no cover page.

Title: UMBRELLA DUTY

SHEVANTABAI (72), a farmer from the village below, with a black umbrella and nowhere else to be.

SAGAR (24), a crop-insurance verifier from Pune, with a raincoat still in its packet.

EXT. WEATHER STATION, DONGARWADI, BEED - AFTERNOON

A hilltop. A solar panel. A rain gauge in a padlocked steel-mesh cage.

Up here the grass is green. Below, brown fields run to the horizon, and a temple loudspeaker prays for rain.

One cloud.

Shevantabai sits on a stone, holding her umbrella over the cage.

Sagar pushes his scooter up the track.

His tablet: STATION SERVES 23 VILLAGES. RAINFALL SINCE 1 JUNE: 3.5 MM. FLAG: VERIFY BEFORE PAYOUT.

He looks at the cloud. At the umbrella. At her.

SAGAR

Namaskar, Aaji.

She nods. The umbrella doesn't.

He takes off his helmet.

One-handed, she unknots a cloth and holds out a bhakri.

SHEVANTABAI

(eyes on the cloud)

Kha.

SAGAR

Duty chalu aahe.

She keeps holding it out. He takes it.

The tablet: STEP 4. PHOTOGRAPH FUNNEL. NO OBSTRUCTIONS.

He glances at the umbrella. She lifts it exactly out of frame.

On screen: a dry funnel, her bare toes at the edge. He steps left until they're gone, shoots, taps SUBMIT. A green tick: FUNNEL DRY.

A fat drop lands on the green tick. Then a thousand more.

She gets wet. The gauge doesn't.

Sagar rips the packet open with his teeth and holds the raincoat over her.

They stay like that: the rain on him, the raincoat on her, the umbrella on the gauge.

The cloud swings out, dragging its tail over the cage. Rain darkens the fields of the village below. The rest stay brown.

The loudspeaker stops mid-prayer.

Shevantabai stands, watching her fields. She opens her hand.

The umbrella cartwheels downhill after the rain.

She follows it, face up, in no hurry.

Sagar holds the raincoat over an empty stone.

The gauge starts to click.

He checks the green tick. He looks down at the other twenty-two villages, still brown. He moves the raincoat over the cage. The clicking stops.

Far below, her umbrella lies upside down in a furrow, filling with rain.

4. Concept

One concept for a feature-length script, narrative film, or documentary you would like to develop. Treatment or prose format.

WINTEROVER (feature drama)

March 1984. Twelve soldiers and scientists begin India's first Antarctic winter at Dakshin Gangotri, a station built in eight weeks. The radio is their only line home. Everything runs on one diesel generator, kept alive by Havildar Malkiat Singh, 41, an army mechanic who sleeps beside it. If it stops, the station freezes in hours.

In June, with the sun gone, the radio reports that the army has stormed the Golden Temple and Sikh soldiers have mutinied. The mess goes quiet when Malkiat walks in. Someone padlocks the fuel store. Major Anand Rao, the commander, keeps the key. Malkiat asks him for it every six hours.

On 31 October Indira Gandhi is shot by two Sikh bodyguards, and Delhi mobs begin hunting Sikhs. The Major announces her death and tells everyone but Malkiat the rest. Malkiat's wife and sons are in east Delhi. The relief ship has not left Goa, and a mechanic thinking about Delhi could freeze them all. The radio operator, Neil Fernandes, edits the bulletins and rations calls home. When Malkiat's turn comes, the Major invents a solar storm.

That night the Major takes Malkiat his dinner and hears the BBC on a transistor behind the generator. Malkiat has been listening since June. He has kept them warm anyway.

The sun stops setting. The ship is still three weeks out, and eleven men share one hut with the man they lied to.

At midnight, in full sun, Malkiat sits alone at the radio, waiting for Delhi to answer. Outside, the other eleven wait on the ice so the hut is his for as long as it takes.

8. Stern Essay 1: Professional aspirations

What are your short-term career goals? Why is the Stern MBA the necessary next chapter in your professional story? Please be specific.

In the camera department, I work inside frames that others have already decided on and paid for. As a camera assistant on Matchbox Studios' Khauf, and since October 2025 for producer Boney Kapoor at Bayview Projects, I have watched the choices made before a shoot, about money, time, places and casting, set the limits of a crew's day. My short-term goal is a role in development and production at a company making films and series for Indian and international audiences, where I can learn how projects are chosen, financed, packaged and sold, and within a few years to build a company of my own.

One project I watched grow was a shoot with a musician from my school. We took photographs on a secluded hilly road behind the city and both liked the results enough to shoot again the next day, a sequence of pictures in which he stays still while the background moves, and then once more, entirely on video. Each round, I now see, was a decision about what the work should become.

Since then I have moved closer to those decisions. At Whistling Woods International, while training as a cinematographer, I shot behind-the-scenes stills and video for many student sets. I have also done work that comes before a camera rolls: the PPM deck, cast references, production sheets and costume trials for a Finolex commercial, on Early Man Productions' direction team, and the plan for a Sleepyhead Mattresses shoot at Growth Jockey, covering the script, the equipment, the audience and the offer. None of this was in a production department, and none of it taught me how media companies make money, how a project is valued and financed, or how to manage a team and a balance sheet at once.

The Stern MBA is the necessary next chapter because my long-term goal is a business: a studio in India, with a team of 8 to 30, that develops original films and series and hires talented people without film-family connections, trains them, pays them fairly and trusts them early. Running it will mean structuring deals, managing cash across long projects, choosing projects and markets and building a team that stays, and I want finance, strategy and leadership training before other people's jobs and money depend on my decisions. The MBA/MFA is built for this: a first year mostly at Stern, then Tisch producing courses in packaging and in the structure and management of an independent film company, and a market course that ends at Cannes.

My school subjects were economics, accountancy and business studies, and as the media manager of Mitti to Matka, a COVID initiative supporting Gurugram's potters, I handled the administration and treated the money as a trust, since it was meant for their families. Stern would turn that groundwork into professional skill in the same years that Tisch trains the craft, so that when I decide what goes into a frame, I can also answer for what it costs and whom it pays.
