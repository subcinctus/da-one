1. Tisch Statement of Purpose

Who you are and why you're applying. In what ways do you plan to contribute to the program and beyond? What are your academic and professional goals? How have your experiences, successes, and failures shaped how you see the world? What kinds of stories do you hope to tell?

Like many boys in 2016, I was obsessed with phones and their cameras, and I practised on our family-trip photographs, editing every one and having them all printed. The first thing I taught myself was the cutout: lifting a subject out, blending in a new background and highlighting the focus in an exaggerated, stylish way. They were contrast frames, built to stand out. Ten years on, I am applying to Tisch Graduate Film and the Stern dual degree to become a producer, by way of three kinds of frame.

Bored of zooming into pictures on a phone, I picked up a camera. After a failed stint with Filmora, a video editor, the camera felt like a new achievement, and I kept at it until there was nothing on it I had not tried. On a family trip to Monaco I taught myself macro, manual HDR blending and long exposures of a changing sky, and caught an F1 car from above, flat out on the straight, dead centre in the frame. Those were balanced frames, all composition and control.

People came next, at the Raghu Rai Center for Photography, where Nitin Rai had spent months teaching me fixed-camera fashion shoots. Then he called in a model for a commercial shoot, and I hid in a corner trying to get better pictures than the Vogue-style studio set-up. I got some beautiful frames, and so did he, and that day he saw I loved a free-moving camera far more.

Then the frame began to move. The Classroom, the web series in this application, had already given me my first real video work, and after it and the photography course I was probably way too confident. In eleventh grade I approached a musician at school whose music had blown my mind, and we shot many, many pictures on a secluded hilly road. He loved them, and the next day, at his request, we shot a sequence of stills in which he stays steady while the background moves. He loved that so much that he would not risk a new location, so we went back and shot it all on video, him driving in and opening the back of the car. The pictures were for someone else now, made to his wishes.

On a professional shoot for SHLAY, a senior's new clothing label, I felt the urge for a third kind of picture, not contrast, not balance, but impact: a frame that meant something. The free-moving camera found its subject at Whistling Woods International, where I trained as a cinematographer. Student sets kept asking me to shoot their behind-the-scenes stills and video, more than I could cover, so I committed to shoots in advance, and on days with no set of my own I still wanted to be on one, for the beautiful sets, the people at work and the candid moments. My camera had turned from the films to the people making them, and my CV lists the films I have shot and assisted on since.

As a camera assistant I keep seeing problems that started earlier, in a schedule, a budget, a location or a casting choice, reach the camera and the people I used to photograph. A producer decides what reaches the frame before anyone rolls. I have not yet worked in a production office.

The pieces in this application show the films I want to make: a father's apology in a Rohtak photo studio, made with twelve photographs and without the word sorry; a farmer's umbrella over a rain gauge in drought-hit Beed; and an army mechanic keeping India's first Antarctic station warm in 1984 while the others hide the Delhi massacre of Sikhs from him. Each follows people at work when something larger reaches them, and those are the stories I want to tell, for audiences in India and the United States.

At Tisch I would bring a cinematographer's eye and experience of Indian sets to an international cohort, and in Introduction to Producing I would produce a classmate's film, as glad to be on someone else's set as I was at Whistling Woods. Shrihari Sathe, Tisch's head of producing, is an Indian-born independent producer whose work has travelled the way I want mine to.

Academically, I want to learn how films are developed, budgeted, scheduled, financed, distributed and marketed, alongside directing and writing. After a few years in development and production, I plan to start a studio in India of 8 to 30 people making its own films and series. I began with frames built to stand out, learned balance under a changing sky and found impact in people at work. If my studio's crews reach the set with problems already solved and time to look for the impact frame, I will have used Tisch well.

2. Dramatic Story

A two-page story using one theme: (a) two characters, one cannot hear the other; (b) survival depends on an unlikely skill; (c) an intruder saves a delicate situation; (d) your older sibling wakes you in the middle of the night and suggests something marginally illegal; (e) at exactly the wrong moment, a character says out loud what they were thinking; (f) a mother makes amends with her child through humor; (g) an apology is made without ever saying sorry.

Bharat Photo Studio in Rohtak still has the painted backdrop of Switzerland, faded to a pale green lake under pale green mountains. Amit has not stood in front of it for nine years.

He comes in at seven in the evening, when the street is loud with scooters and frying. He has a baby on his shoulder, a passport appointment at ten the next morning and a flight to Toronto in five weeks. Every studio in Delhi he called said three days. His father's studio has always said one hour.

Harish is sixty-eight and doesn't get up from behind the counter. He looks at the baby, then at the clock.

"Name?"

"Zoya."

Harish writes it on an envelope in the slow capitals he uses for customers. He doesn't ask for the surname. Nine years ago he said he would not come to the wedding, and he didn't, and Farzana's family hired a man from Panipat who cut off the tops of everyone's heads.

He pulls the white roll down over Switzerland. He seats Amit on the stool with the baby on his knees and drapes a white towel over Amit's hands so only she shows. He sets the lights the way he always has, the left one higher, the umbrella tilted. Then he crouches, which Amit has never seen him do, so the camera is at the baby's height.

Zoya won't open her eyes. Harish makes the sound he used to make on school photo days, a click of the tongue and a short whistle. Her eyes open. He shoots. He shoots again.

"One is enough," Amit says.

"For the passport."

Amit waits on the bench while the printer warms up. He did his homework on this bench as a boy, under other people's weddings framed along the walls. The walls haven't changed. Brides from 1987 and 1994. A police inspector with a moustache. His own Class 4 school photo, still in the corner.

Harish comes back with the prints, eight small faces on a glossy sheet, and a second envelope, older, soft at the corners. He puts both on the counter and goes back to switch off the lights.

Inside the old envelope are twelve photographs of a garden in Delhi on a winter night. Amit knows the garden. He knows the marigold arch, the cheap tent, the DJ. Every frame is taken from far behind the crowd on a long lens, between strangers' shoulders, past a pillar, through a gap in the tent wall. In one, Farzana is laughing at something Amit has said. In another, the two of them are tiny at the end of an aisle of plastic chairs. The light is good. The focus is perfect. They are printed on the studio's best paper, and they have never been taken out.

At the back of the shop, Harish is coiling cables and doesn't turn around.

Amit lays the baby on the counter, on the soft envelope. She grabs a photograph and holds on.

"How much?" Amit asks, about the passport prints.

"Sit," his father says. "I'll take a bigger one. For the wall."

He rolls the white paper up, and behind them, Switzerland comes back.

3. Short Scene

A cinematic scene between two people that reveals something about the characters. One-sentence description of each character allowed; essential details only; no backstory but incorporate behavior; no cover page.

Title: UMBRELLA DUTY

SHEVANTABAI (72), a farmer from the village below, with a black umbrella and nowhere else to be.

SAGAR (24), a crop-insurance verifier from Pune, with a raincoat still in its packet.

EXT. WEATHER STATION, DONGARWADI, BEED - AFTERNOON

A hilltop. A solar panel. A rain gauge in a padlocked steel-mesh cage.

Up here the grass is green. Below, brown fields run to the horizon, and a temple loudspeaker prays for rain.

One cloud.

Shevantabai sits on a stone, holding her umbrella over the cage.

Sagar pushes his scooter up the track.

His tablet: STATION SERVES 23 VILLAGES. RAINFALL SINCE 1 JUNE: 3.5 MM. FLAG: VERIFY BEFORE PAYOUT.

He looks at the cloud. At the umbrella. At her.

SAGAR

Namaskar, Aaji.

She nods. The umbrella doesn't.

He takes off his helmet.

One-handed, she unknots a cloth and holds out a bhakri.

SHEVANTABAI

(eyes on the cloud)

Kha.

SAGAR

Duty chalu aahe.

She keeps holding it out. He takes it.

The tablet: STEP 4. PHOTOGRAPH FUNNEL. NO OBSTRUCTIONS.

He glances at the umbrella. She lifts it exactly out of frame.

On screen: a dry funnel, her bare toes at the edge. He steps left until they're gone, shoots, taps SUBMIT. A green tick: FUNNEL DRY.

A fat drop lands on the green tick. Then a thousand more.

She gets wet. The gauge doesn't.

Sagar rips the packet open with his teeth and holds the raincoat over her.

They stay like that: the rain on him, the raincoat on her, the umbrella on the gauge.

The cloud swings out, dragging its tail over the cage. Rain darkens the fields of the village below. The rest stay brown.

The loudspeaker stops mid-prayer.

Shevantabai stands, watching her fields. She opens her hand.

The umbrella cartwheels downhill after the rain.

She follows it, face up, in no hurry.

Sagar holds the raincoat over an empty stone.

The gauge starts to click.

He checks the green tick. He looks down at the other twenty-two villages, still brown. He moves the raincoat over the cage. The clicking stops.

Far below, her umbrella lies upside down in a furrow, filling with rain.

4. Concept

One concept for a feature-length script, narrative film, or documentary you would like to develop. Treatment or prose format.

WINTEROVER (feature drama)

March 1984. Twelve soldiers and scientists begin India's first Antarctic winter at Dakshin Gangotri, a station built in eight weeks. The radio is their only line home. Everything runs on one diesel generator, kept alive by Havildar Malkiat Singh, 41, an army mechanic who sleeps beside it. If it stops, the station freezes in hours.

In June, with the sun gone, the radio reports that the army has stormed the Golden Temple and Sikh soldiers have mutinied. The mess goes quiet when Malkiat walks in. Someone padlocks the fuel store. Major Anand Rao, the commander, keeps the key. Malkiat asks him for it every six hours.

On 31 October Indira Gandhi is shot by two Sikh bodyguards, and Delhi mobs begin hunting Sikhs. The Major announces her death and tells everyone but Malkiat the rest. Malkiat's wife and sons are in east Delhi. The relief ship has not left Goa, and a mechanic thinking about Delhi could freeze them all. The radio operator, Neil Fernandes, edits the bulletins and rations calls home. When Malkiat's turn comes, the Major invents a solar storm.

That night the Major takes Malkiat his dinner and hears the BBC on a transistor behind the generator. Malkiat has been listening since June. He has kept them warm anyway.

The sun stops setting. The ship is still three weeks out, and eleven men share one hut with the man they lied to.

At midnight, in full sun, Malkiat sits alone at the radio, waiting for Delhi to answer. Outside, the other eleven wait on the ice so the hut is his for as long as it takes.

8. Stern Essay 1: Professional aspirations

What are your short-term career goals? Why is the Stern MBA the necessary next chapter in your professional story? Please be specific.

On family trips to Dehradun I shot on a Canon 600D with its kit lens, then traded it for a nifty fifty on a 7D Mark II. The pairing opened possibilities that a better camera could not have offered: I used manual focus creatively for the first time, played with bokeh and learned how stabilisers work, which led me to ask why the 7D had no in-body stabilisation when Sony offered it for less. I was asking about price, product and value before I had the words, and the company I want to build will depend on all three.

My CV lists the sets I have worked on, most recently as a camera assistant on a project under producer Boney Kapoor. My short-term goal is a development role at a company that makes films and shows for Indian and international audiences, where I would learn how projects are chosen, financed, packaged and sold before I build a company of my own.

That company is why the Stern MBA is the necessary next chapter. I plan it as an Indian studio, 8 to 30 people strong, that develops its own slate and hires talented people without film-family connections, trains them, pays them fairly and trusts them early. Running it will mean structuring deals, managing cash across long projects, choosing projects and markets and building a team that stays. Film school taught me how a film is made, not how a project is valued and financed, or how to manage a team and a balance sheet at once, and I want a grounding in finance and leadership before other people's jobs and money depend on my decisions.

The MBA/MFA is built for this: a first year mostly at Stern, then Tisch producing courses in packaging and in the structure and management of an independent film company, and a market course that ends at Cannes, where films are bought and sold. I would learn the business in the same years that Tisch trains the craft.

My school subjects were economics, accountancy and business studies, so some of the groundwork is there. I started by asking why a cheaper camera had a feature mine lacked. At Stern I would learn to ask the same questions of a film: what it costs, who pays for it and whom it pays.
