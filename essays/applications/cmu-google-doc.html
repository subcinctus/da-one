1. Career goals

What are your career goals after completing graduate school?

After graduating I plan to work in development and production at a company that makes films and series for Indian and international audiences, in a role where I can learn how projects are chosen, financed, packaged and sold. Working in the camera department has taught me to trace a problem backwards: when something goes wrong at the camera, it has usually started earlier, in a schedule, a budget, a location or a casting choice, and the camera team can only work around it. I want to be where those choices are made.

After a few years in that work, I plan to build a creative studio in India of 8 to 30 people that develops its own slate of films and series, including projects that can travel between India and the United States. It would be small enough for every project to get real attention, and run as a sound business, with clear budgets, fair contracts and a slate strategy that survives the years between hits. It would also give paid roles to talented people with no family in the industry, train them on the job and trust them with responsibility. Nobody at home expected my camera to become a career; my parents did not talk to us about work until my senior years at school. If the studio makes work that earns its audience in India and abroad, and gives even a few people a way in that does not depend on family, it will have been worth building.

2. Prior experience

How have your prior experiences (professional, academic, and/or personal) prepared you for graduate study and informed your career goals?

In class at Suncity School I studied economics, business studies and accountancy; outside it, from 2016, I edited every photograph from our family trips on my phone and had them all printed. The first thing I taught myself was object selection: cutting a subject free, setting it against a new background and highlighting the focus in an exaggerated, stylish way. I call those contrast frames, built to stand out.

Bored of zooming into pictures, I picked up a camera, and a family trip to Monaco taught me the balanced frame: I waited out long exposures while the sky changed, followed seagulls soaring about fifty metres off with an 18-135mm at full zoom, and caught an F1 car flat out on the straight, from above, dead centre in the frame.

At the Raghu Rai Center for Photography, where I studied under Nitin Rai, my camera turned to people. When he brought in a model for a commercial shoot, I hid in a corner, hunting for better pictures than the Vogue-style studio set-up would give. I came away with some beautiful frames, and so did he. He saw, after months of teaching me fixed-camera fashion shoots, that I loved a free-moving camera far more.

After The Classroom, my stills began to move for someone else: a musician at my school whose music blew my mind. Our first shoot, on a secluded hilly road outside the city, gave us many, many pictures. At his request we shot again the very next day: a sequence of stills in which he stays steady while the background moves. He loved it too much to risk a new location, so we went back and shot only video: him driving in and opening the back of the car. Each shoot was shaped by what he wanted from the last.

It was not until a professional shoot for SHLAY, a senior's new clothing label, that I felt the urge for a third kind of picture: neither a contrast frame nor a balanced one, but an impact frame, one that meant something. At Whistling Woods International, where I trained as a cinematographer, student crews asked me to shoot their behind-the-scenes stills and video, more than I could cover, so I committed to shoots in advance, and on days without a set of my own I still wanted to be on one, for beautiful sets, people at work and candid moments. I was photographing the people making films, not the films, and those were the frames that meant something. Somebody had put each set together before I arrived, and I wanted to be that somebody.

My CV lists the sets I have worked on, most recently as a camera assistant at Bayview Projects under producer Boney Kapoor. Each stage gave me a new frame, and the last takes in the whole set and everyone on it: the producer's frame. Heinz is where my commerce classroom and my camera finally meet, and where I would learn to compose it.

3. Why Heinz College

How will attending this degree program at Heinz College help you achieve your career goals?

I traded my first camera and its kit lens for a Canon 7D Mark II and a nifty fifty, a plain 50mm lens, and the pair opened possibilities a better camera on its own could not have. Soon I wanted to know why Sony could sell in-body stabilisation for less when my camera had none. Even then, I was asking business questions. Film school taught me how a film is made, but not how to use data and financial analysis to decide which film to make, and that gap is the one between a cinematographer and a producer.

MEIM would teach me that side of the work in the order I need it. In Pittsburgh I would build the foundations I lack, in production finance, script and story analysis, and negotiation. In Los Angeles I would apply them in production management, film economics, business development and intellectual property, while interning in the industry alongside classes and meeting the people and companies who could later become partners. The capstone, a team project for a real industry client, would let me work on that client's business problem before I run a company of my own.

Before a trip to France I built my own colour profiles, and once there I never felt the need to edit a frame, because the decisions had been made before I left. Heinz would teach me to make a project's decisions that early, with numbers behind choosing a slate, pricing a project and understanding an audience. In return I would bring set experience in India and a cinematographer's sense of what each decision costs by the time it reaches the screen.

4. Optional essay

Address gaps in professional experience or application weaknesses.

My undergraduate degree is a B.Sc. in Filmmaking, Cinematography and Direction from Whistling Woods International in Mumbai, awarded by the Tata Institute of Social Sciences. It is a three-year programme, the standard length for this degree in India, and I completed it in 2025.

My professional experience, during the degree and since, has been mostly on set, so I have not yet worked in a production office. The nearest I have come to one is direction-team work on a Finolex commercial for Early Man Productions, where I prepared the production sheets, cast references and pre-production meeting deck and ran costume trials. For Growth Jockey I planned a Sleepyhead Mattresses shoot: the equipment, the script, and the offer and audience the film was aimed at.

I have learned a craft from the beginning before, on my own. My first try at video editing, a stint with Filmora, failed, and when a camera came along later, I taught myself until there was nothing on it I had not tried. The business side of film is one I want to learn properly, with teachers this time, and that is why I am applying to MEIM.
