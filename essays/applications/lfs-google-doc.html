1. Treatment or synopsis

A short treatment/synopsis for a feature film or television drama series that you would like to produce. Why do you want to make this project, with reference to its subject, genre, characters, plot, themes and who you envisage as its audience(s)?

REGISTERED POST: an original feature film, a dramedy in Hindi, Punjabi and English

Haridwar, 4 a.m. Vasu Kaushik, 26, stands knee-deep in the Ganga with a stranger's ashes in one hand and a selfie stick in the other, so a family in New Jersey can watch on WhatsApp. For seven generations his family has kept handwritten registers of births, marriages and deaths for villages in Punjab's Doaba belt. The villages have emptied into Brampton, Southall and California, and the dead now come home by post.

A brass urn arrives from Dayton, Ohio, with one instruction: don't put me in the river until you write me back into the ledger your grandfather struck me from. In the 1974 Hoshiarpur register Vasu finds Darshan Lal Saini, struck through, and in the margin, "Gone to America with the American." The American is Gene Becker, 80, Darshan's widower, who made the urn himself. In ten days Darshan's nephews arrive from Brampton with their father's ashes to sign the same page. Surinder, who is selling the family land, fears a claim; his brother Bunty knew their father wrote to Darshan every month and never said so. Vasu's father, Pandit Ramesh Kaushik, says the ledger is not theirs to edit, and does not mention that in 1974, aged six, he held the blotting paper.

At the signing Vasu props his phone on the trunk and calls Ohio. Gene reads from fifty years of their father's letters, every one addressed to both men. Bunty breaks first, and Surinder declares the entry as head of the family. Vasu writes Darshan back in, with Gene beside him as jeevan saathi, life companion, and his father blots the line himself.

I want to make it because it is about who a family allows to count. A struck line leaves the name legible and removes the person, and the film corrects it by the register's own rules, the way the accountancy I studied at school corrects a ledger: never by rubbing out, always with a new entry. The comedy lives in the logistics, never in the grief.

Its audience is the families on both ends of those calls, in India, the United Kingdom, Canada and the United States, reached through festivals. The budget is modest: Haridwar locations, a small cast and a few days in Ohio, with Gene as an American co-lead written for an India-US co-production.

3. Why you would make a good producer

A brief explanation of why you think you will make a good producer.

At film school my camera pointed at the people making films rather than at the films. At Whistling Woods International, where I trained as a cinematographer, student sets kept asking me to shoot their behind-the-scenes stills and video, more sets than I could cover, so I committed to shoots in advance, and even on free days I wanted to be on someone else's set. As a camera assistant on Khauf at Matchbox Studios and now at Bayview Projects under producer Boney Kapoor, I have seen that most problems reaching the frame were decided earlier, in the schedule, the budget, the locations or the cast. A producer makes those decisions, and I want to make them well. I have done small first versions of the work: organising The Classroom, a web series made with school friends, around our exams; preparing production sheets, cast references and a PPM deck in the Finolex direction team; and handling money meant for Gurugram's potters at Mitti to Matka. I studied commerce before film, so I read a budget as closely as a frame. I have not yet worked in a production office, and that is what I am coming to London to learn.

4. Producing perspective (one of three options)

Choose one: a recent feature film or TV drama series that you would love to have produced, discussed from a professional producing perspective; OR a producer or showrunner whose work you respect professionally, and why; OR recommend an online resource (e.g. video masterclass, podcast, production software, social media platform) and explain how and why you believe it to be of exceptional use for your future producing career.

Option chosen: a producer I respect.

The producer whose work I respect most is Guneet Monga. She came to Indian cinema with no film background, starting in Delhi on other people's films before founding Sikhya Entertainment in Mumbai. What I study in her career is how a small company carries very local stories to audiences far outside India.

The Lunchbox is the clearest example. A lunch delivered to the wrong Mumbai office starts letters between two strangers, and the film never raises its voice. As a producer, I look at how it was built: an India, France, Germany and US co-production that premiered in the Critics' Week at Cannes in 2013, won its Viewers' Choice Award, and went out to the world through an international sales agent. Masaan, another Sikhya film, won a critics' prize at Cannes two years later, and Monga then took a short documentary, The Elephant Whisperers, to the first Oscar for an Indian production.

Each of those films depended on a producer matching a specific story to the right partners, the right festival and the right audience. That is the work I cannot yet do, which is why the Story, Script and Commissioning and the Finance, Business Affairs and Rights Management modules matter most to me at LFS. Monga had no film family behind her, and neither do I. I want to build a studio in India of 8 to 30 people on the same principle: specific Indian stories, financed well enough to travel.
