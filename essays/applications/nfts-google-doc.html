1. Project idea (film or HETV drama)

An idea for a film or HETV drama that you would like to produce, with a synopsis and your ideal director, writer and lead actor. The idea should be based on source material set out in a book, play, documentary, historical event or article; you do not need to have secured an option on the rights for the application.

BORROWED SKY: a period drama series for HETV, eight episodes of about 50 minutes

Source material. A historical event: the Satellite Instructional Television Experiment, known as SITE, which ran from 1 August 1975 to 31 July 1976. NASA lent India its ATS-6 satellite for one year, and ISRO used it to beam farming, health and family-planning programmes to community televisions in about 2,400 villages, each fed by a ten-foot dish of chicken wire. The broadcasts began five weeks into Indira Gandhi's Emergency, whose sterilisation drive is the second strand of the story. Both are public history, so no option is needed; the characters are invented.

Logline. In 1975 an ISRO technician rides a motorcycle between forty Orissa villages, keeping their first televisions alive, until Emergency officials use the evening viewings to round up men for sterilisation and she starts breaking her own sets.

Synopsis. In Sambalpur, Orissa, Jharana Meher, 23, a weaver's daughter and radio mechanic, is the only woman on the satellite maintenance team, and her wage is the first in her house not earned at a loom. She finds a fault in a circuit by smell and one in a village by watching who sits where. Her supervisor, Thomas Varghese, a Keralite who wants Orissa's uptime to beat Rajasthan's, hired her over eleven men, and by July he must recommend one technician for a permanent post.

Each episode a different set goes dead, Jharana rides out on her Rajdoot, and the fault is never in the set. It is a priest who believes the screen steals his worshippers, or a caste quarrel over who sits in front. In the pilot, on the first day of broadcasts, Kendupali's picture is all snow because a nine-year-old goatherd, Gopal Majhi, has turned the dish towards the sunrise and garlanded it with marigolds, since gods face east. Ed Lindqvist, a NASA engineer riding pillion, tries to explain geostationary orbit with a lantern and a guava. Jharana tells the boy that this god is only visiting, like Jagannath riding to his aunt's house, and a good host faces his guest. Gopal helps her swing the dish west, the marigolds stay, and three hundred people applaud a test card.

Two clocks run underneath. The first is the loan: on 31 July 1976 the satellite goes home, and Jharana's job with it. The second is the district's sterilisation target. Dr Ashok Rath, a courteous family-planning officer who has delivered enough ninth children to believe in his programme, receives larger targets from Delhi every month, and he notices what the evening broadcast does: it puts every man in a village in one place at dusk. Nilamani Sahu, Kendupali's headmaster, who wears the television key round his neck and oils his hair before broadcasts in case the screen can see him, is given a quota of men to motivate.

By spring the round-ups begin. Jharana, who has spent a year keeping the sets alive, starts breaking them so the men stay home, and she cannot say why without risking jail. Varghese sees Orissa's uptime falling and his best technician's name on every failure. Ed has orders to report on the hardware and stay out of Indian politics. The season ends the night the sky goes blank, when the loan ends and every screen in the district goes dark at once, the ones she saved and the ones she broke.

Ideal team. As director, Vikramaditya Motwane, who directed Jubilee for Prime Video, a ten-episode period drama set in the Bombay film studios around Independence that makes old machines, and the people who depend on them, feel urgent. As writer, Atul Sabharwal, who wrote the screenplay and dialogue for Jubilee and shaped a large ensemble across a full season, which is what the Kendupali villagers, the maintenance centre and the district office need. As Jharana, Preeti Panigrahi, who made her debut as the lead of Girls Will Be Girls and won the Special Jury Award for Acting at Sundance in 2024, with a jury citation praising a character played with "intelligence and vulnerability". Jharana needs both: she must be the cleverest person in every village she rides into, and the one with the most to lose.

Why I want to produce it. My own work began with making pictures for my family, and this story is about villages seeing their first picture on a screen, a test card that three hundred people applaud. Behind that wonder is a question I have learned to ask on set as a camera assistant: who decided what reached the frame, and for whose benefit. The series lets a period drama turn into a thriller without inventing either half, because the satellite and the Emergency really did share that year.

As a production, it is contained. The forty villages can be built from a few real ones in western Odisha, dressed for 1975, and the repeated elements, the dishes, the motorcycle and the maintenance centre, are made once and used across the season. A single American role and a satellite lent by NASA give it a natural audience outside India, and its first home would be a streaming platform that has already commissioned period series of this scale. I have not yet developed, financed or scheduled a series, and BORROWED SKY is the project I would most like to learn those skills on.

2. Favourite film or TV drama

Your favourite film or TV drama: name it, explain why you chose it, and assess both its strengths and its weaknesses.

My favourite film is The Lunchbox (2013), written and directed by Ritesh Batra. I chose it because it trusts small things to carry a whole story: a tiffin sent to the wrong desk, notes tucked into the lunch, and two people, a widowed claims clerk near retirement played by Irrfan Khan and a neglected young wife played by Nimrat Kaur, who never share a scene.

Its greatest strength is restraint. The camera stays close to routine, the crowded commuter trains, a kitchen at dawn, an office of ledgers, and lets the letters do the emotional work, so a change in a lonely man's manners lands harder than any speech could. The voice-only neighbour upstairs is another quiet gift, a whole character built from sound and a basket on a rope. As a producer, I also admire how the film was put together across India, France, Germany and the United States, which carried a story about Mumbai's lunch carriers to Cannes and to cinemas abroad.

Its weaknesses come from the same restraint. The husband is written as an absence rather than a person, which makes the heroine's choice easier than it should be. The plot rests on a mistake by a delivery system famous for not making them, and the film leans on that coincidence more than once. The open ending feels earned to me, but some viewers will feel the letters stop just when the story asks for a decision.

3. Two British producers or HETV executives you admire

Two British producers or HETV executives, known primarily in that role, whose work you admire, in 75 words each.

Christian Colson. He produced Slumdog Millionaire, adapted from Vikas Swarup's novel Q & A and shot in Mumbai, and won the Academy Award for Best Picture. Accepting the Producers Guild's award, he noted that some had argued the film should never have been made, with no major stars and dialogue partly in Hindi. He then produced 127 Hours, from Aron Ralston's memoir. I admire how he turns source material into films that travel.

Jane Featherstone. At Kudos she rose from head of drama to chief executive, executive producing Spooks and Life on Mars, then founded Sister Pictures. Through Sister she executive produced Chernobyl for HBO and Sky, a true disaster told through procedure and the people inside it. It is the closest model I know for BORROWED SKY: a real event from decades ago, with the tension of a thriller.

4. Reasons for applying (application form)

Your reasons for applying, given in the NFTS online application form.

Long before I worked on a set, I was making pictures, first what I now call contrast frames, family photographs edited until they stood out, and then, on a course in fashion and advertising photography under Nitin Rai at the Raghu Rai Center for Photography in Gurugram, the impact frame: pictures that matter to the people in them. What I had not yet asked was who decides what reaches a frame at all.

The frame began to move when a shoot with a musician from school went from stills on a hilly road behind the city to a sequence that was all video. At school I also made The Classroom with friends, a five-part web series for YouTube; I shared the directing, and shot and edited it. I organised it too, finding places to shoot, fitting shoots around our exams and keeping classmates involved from one episode to the next.

My formal training came at Whistling Woods International in Mumbai, where I studied cinematography for a B.Sc. in Filmmaking, Cinematography and Direction, a TISS degree completed in 2025. There my camera turned round. Classmates kept asking me to cover their shoots from behind the scenes, in stills and on video, and the requests outran the days I had, so I had to promise shoots in advance, and when I had no set of my own I still wanted to be on one. Photographing every department at work taught me that a film is mostly made of decisions taken before anyone rolls.

My working life since has confirmed it. Assisting the camera team on Khauf at Matchbox Studios under Pankaj Kumar, and since October 2025 working as a camera assistant for producer Boney Kapoor's Bayview Projects, I have watched problems arrive at the camera that began weeks earlier, in a schedule, a budget, a location or a casting choice. On the direction team of a Finolex commercial for Early Man Productions I prepared the production sheets, cast references and PPM deck and ran costume trials. For Growth Jockey I planned a Sleepyhead Mattresses shoot, from the equipment and script to the offer and the audience it was aimed at. As media manager of Mitti to Matka, which supported Gurugram's potters during COVID, I handled money their families depended on, and I came to film from a commerce stream of economics, business studies and accountancy.

I have never worked in a production office, optioned a story or raised money for a film. The MFA Producing course is built around exactly those stages: identifying story ideas and securing the rights to them, creative development with writers and directors, business affairs and film and television financing, and physical producing through the shoot and into post. Above all, I want to produce at least three short films to industry standard with teams of NFTS students from the other disciplines. I have spent years beside directors and cinematographers; at NFTS I would learn to stand behind them and answer for their films, with a mentor from the industry in the second year to test my judgement against.

After graduating I want to spend some years in development and production, then build a studio in India, between 8 and 30 people strong, that develops its own films and series, careful with money and open to talented people with no family in the industry. On every set, the camera team is looking for the impact frame. I want to be the producer who makes sure that, when they find it, there is a good story, enough time and an honest budget in front of the lens.
