1. Statement of Purpose

What is your purpose in applying for graduate study in your specified degree program? Describe your area(s) of interest. What experiences have prepared you for advanced study in this program? What relevant skills have you gained? Have your experiences led to specific or tangible outcomes (performances, publications, presentations, awards or recognitions)? What additional information about your past experience may aid the committee (research, employment, teaching, service, artistic or international experiences developing leadership, communication, project management, teamwork)? Why is the UCLA graduate program the best place for you to pursue your academic goals? What are your plans for your career after earning this degree? (UCLA TFT also asks: give the faculty a sense of who you are; what you hope to achieve in the program; your professional goals and how you plan to achieve them.)

I am applying to the Producers Program to shape films before they reach the camera. My eye got there one frame at a time. In 2016 I was obsessed with phones and their cameras. My editing went from filters to graphic elements, and the first thing I taught myself was object selection: cutting a subject out, blending in a new background and highlighting the focus in an exaggerated, stylish way. Those were contrast frames, built to stand out.

A camera taught me balance. On a family trip to Monaco I learned macro, manual HDR blending and long exposures of a changing sky, the symmetrical, balanced frames of landscapes. I caught an F1 car flat out on the straight, shot from above and dead centre in the frame, and, with an 18-135mm at full zoom, seagulls soaring about fifty metres away.

By the time I shot professionally for SHLAY, a senior's new boutique clothing brand, I had moved to a Nikon Z6 with a heavy 24-70, and I had been sure the camera was doing the work until I handed it to someone else and saw that knowledge made the difference. On that shoot I wanted a meaningful frame: not a contrast frame, not a balanced frame, but an impact frame.

My frames had already started to move. In 11th grade a musician at school blew my mind with his music, and we shot many, many pictures on a secluded hilly road outside the city. He loved them, and the next day asked for a sequence of stills in which he stays steady while the background moves. He loved that so much he would not risk a new location, so we went back, and this time it was all video: him driving in, opening the back of the car, a graphic title at the top.

At Whistling Woods International, where I trained as a cinematographer, more student sets wanted me as their behind-the-scenes photographer than I could get to, so I gave my word in advance. My lens had turned from the film to the people making it.

On the sets in my CV, as cinematographer or camera assistant, I keep seeing problems reach the camera that began in earlier decisions, often in a budget line nobody owned. I want to be where those decisions are made. I have not yet worked in a production office. My interest is development, working with a writer while story, budget and audience are still open, and the Producers Program's thesis, a full plan to package, finance, budget, market and distribute one project, is the work I most want to learn.

After a few years in development and production, I plan to build a studio in India of 8 to 30 people with its own slate of films and series. If its crews can look for the impact frame instead of working around problems nobody owned, it will be doing what I came to UCLA to learn.

2. Personal Statement

(a) Are there educational, personal, cultural, economic, or social experiences, not described in your Statement of Purpose, that have shaped your academic journey? If so, how? Have any of these experiences provided unique perspectives you would contribute to your program, field or profession? (b) Describe challenges or barriers you have faced in your pursuit of higher education. What motivated you to persist, and how did you overcome them? (c) What leadership experiences, community outreach, service initiatives, or research projects have you participated in or plan to pursue that aim to positively impact others or the broader community? (d) Describe ways in which your perspectives, experiences, or aspirations have aligned with UCLA's Principles of Community.

In Indian film many people start through family; nobody in mine works in film or photography. My parents never exactly knew that this would become my career, since work was not something they talked to us about until my senior years at school. What they did know was that I was the one who edited every photograph from our family trips and had every one of them printed.

Only when I got bored of zooming into pictures on a phone did I pick up a camera. I had one failed stint behind me, with the video editor Filmora, so the camera felt like a new achievement, and I kept teaching myself until there was nothing on it I had not tried. On trips to Dehradun, where my sibling and two cousins were at school at Hopetown, I shot with a Canon 600D and its kit lens, and later traded it in for a nifty fifty on a 7D Mark II, a combination that opened possibilities beyond what a better camera could offer. With it I used manual focus creatively for the first time, played with bokeh, learned how stabilisers work and wondered why Sony built one into the body for less than mine had cost. There was nobody to ask but the camera itself.

Today our family's houses are full of my polished prints. I sometimes miss the amateur auto-exposure moments when everyone was made to smile, but I prefer candids now, because now the images bring smiles. The career my parents did not see coming had been on their walls all along.

While I was still at school, during COVID, I joined Mitti to Matka, a Gurugram initiative for local potters whose sales had collapsed. We bought and auctioned their pots at fair prices, worked with them on designs and got food and sanitary supplies to their families. I was the media manager and handled the administration, so buyers saw the pots through my photographs and I saw where the money went. A picture of mine could decide what a family earned that week, and every rupee we spent badly was a rupee a family did not receive. It also showed me where our help stopped: we could sell pots and deliver supplies, but we could not undo what the lockdown had done to a whole community.

The studio I want to build in India should train talented people without connections, pay them fairly and trust them with responsibility, because I taught myself with nobody to show me how, and I know what one early chance can be worth. I see the same values in UCLA's Principles of Community, and I want to practise them as a producer: treating shared money as a trust, and saying plainly what a project can and cannot do for the people it touches. If that studio lets in even a few people who, like me, had no one in the industry to call, it will have been worth building.

3. Why the UCLA Producers Program

Why specifically do you want to attend the UCLA Producers Program?

I want the Producers Program because it starts with development, for features and television, and by the spring of the first year I would be pitching a slate of projects to an industry panel. A trip to France taught me why that order matters. I could not stop shooting, and it was not the architecture or the camera that kept me going. I had built my colour profiles before travelling, so I shot on autopilot: motion long exposures, frozen water, birds, strangers, my own family as if on black-and-white film, and I never felt the need to edit a frame. The work done before the trip made the trip. Development is that work at the scale of a film.

The first year also covers entertainment law and international finance and distribution, and I need both: the projects I want to develop should work in India and the United States, so I must understand the contracts and the money on each side. I also want to produce a directing student's thesis film, because I learn best building something with a director from the first conversation.

Los Angeles matters too. As a film student I wanted to be on a set even on days I had none, for the beautiful sets, the people at work and the candid moments. In Los Angeles I want to see what independent producers do between sets and how they keep a slate moving, and to meet people who could become partners when I am producing in India.

4. Qualities in role models

What two to three qualities do you most admire in people you consider role models?

I admire people who notice what someone else loves, even when it is not what they set out to teach. At the Raghu Rai Center for Photography, Nitin Rai had spent months teaching me fixed-camera fashion shoots, and he saw that I loved media creation and a free-moving camera far more. On one assignment, when he had called in a model for a commercial shoot, I hid in a corner trying to get better pictures than the Vogue-style studio set-up.

I also admire people who keep doing the work themselves, and do it well. That day I got some beautiful frames, and so did he.

In my last month there I invited a friend to model for me. She was a complete amateur, and I covered three outfits in two hours. I left probably way too confident, but sure of the kind of person I wanted to work for, and the kind I want to be when the crew is mine.

5. A difficult or stressful situation with collaborators

Briefly describe a specific difficult or stressful situation where you helped solve a problem with collaborators.

At school a group of friends and I made The Classroom, a five-part web series released on YouTube. I co-directed, shot and edited it, and as its organiser I scheduled the shoots around our exams.

On one shoot day, one of the actors dropped out at the last second. Rather than lose the day, we invented a new character on the spot, a funny sports teacher, to replace the missing role, and I had to play him myself.

Our scriptwriter was at home, not on set, and the lines had been written for the role we had lost, so we rewrote them together remotely to suit a sports teacher, and for the costume I got a standard tracksuit at no cost. We lost an actor and kept the day, and the story of the episode still held together.

6. Original Treatment 1 (feature film)

Original treatment (no adaptations). Feature film treatment: title, logline, genre, story synopsis.

Title: MARRYING THE ICE

Logline: A Balti homestay hustler with a one-way ticket to Bengaluru must first help his eighty-year-old grandmother marry two glaciers and revive their village's dead stream, but the groom ice lies across the Line of Control, above the village where her brother has lived since 1971.

Genre: Comic drama

Synopsis:

Ali Hussain, 24, meets every tourist taxi that reaches Turtuk, a Balti apricot village in Ladakh's Nubra Valley that was in Pakistan-administered Baltistan until 1971. He sells rooms in his grandmother's homestay and, at the Thang viewpoint, fifty-rupee looks through binoculars at Frano, the first village across the Line of Control. He does not mention that his great-uncle lives there. His one-way ticket, for the week the season ends, is to a Bengaluru call centre that sells Himalayan holidays.

In August the stream in the stone channels under the apricot trees stops. Zainab Bano, 80, has never seen it dry. The white glacier that feeds it is down to a dirty thumbnail. Zainab tells Ali he is not leaving until the stream runs again. Her father knew the Balti remedy: marry the ice. White female ice and grey male ice, cut from two glaciers, are carried uphill in silence, never set down or stopped, and buried together under boulders, where in ten winters or so a glacier grows. Ali's counter-offer: he marries the ice before his flight, then goes. Zainab agrees at once, which worries him.

In October, Ali cuts the bride from the dying glacier and packs her in apricot crates with sawdust. Only then does Zainab name the groom: Turtuk's ice has always married the grey glacier above Frano, and has not since 1971. The bride cannot stop, so she rides in Ali's taxi, which drives the road to Thang and back, day and night.

The legal route to Frano goes through Delhi, Dubai and Islamabad. When the diesel runs out at 2 a.m., Ali keeps the bride moving on a tractor inner tube in the eddy below the village, the rope round his wrist, until he falls asleep. By morning his own binoculars find the inner tube on the shingle below Frano. "She has gone to her in-laws," says Zainab.

A cousin in Dubai patches through a call from Frano. Sajjad Ahmed, 22, found the crates, stencilled with the homestay's number, and took them to his grandfather, Muhammad Yusuf, 84, Zainab's brother. The siblings hear each other for the first time since 1971: he was at a wedding in Frano the night the armies stopped. They talk about apricots for forty minutes before anyone mentions water. Ali, who sells Frano for fifty rupees a look, holds the phone and says nothing. Frano's stream is dying too, and the village needs a bride: the one on the shingle touched the ground, so she is only ice.

Nothing crosses the Line by hand except the sweets the two armies swap at the brigade's Diwali flag meeting, two days before Ali's flight. Ali talks Major Deepak Sangwan, 36, into adding a box from Turtuk's families to the brigade's sweets, then tells him what will be inside. The Major's register has a column for sweets and none for ice, and he is not going to rule a new one. Across the Line, Sajjad does the same.

At dawn on Diwali, Turtuk cuts two brides, one for Frano and one for Frano's groom. One goes into a sweet box; the men walk the other in circles under the apricot trees, waiting for the groom. At the Line a jawan, briefed on the rules, marks time through twenty minutes of handshakes and photographs with thirty-five kilos of bride on his shoulder, facing a Pakistani soldier doing the same with the groom. The boxes change shoulders without touching the table. Zainab watches through Ali's binoculars. On a roof in Frano, a hand mirror flashes.

That night Ali carries the groom up a north-facing slope and says nothing for six hours, a personal record. A maulvi recites as bride and groom go under the boulders with charcoal, sawdust and clay pots of Shyok water. At the same hour Frano buries Turtuk's bride with its own grey ice. Two glaciers marry, one on each side.

Ali flies to Bengaluru on the date on his ticket, charcoal still under his nails. The binocular money buys a return for next Diwali, when the boxes will carry apricots. Someone has to talk the Major round.

Ten summers later the first meltwater comes down the stone channel. Zainab, ninety, puts her feet in it and tells Ali it is colder than it used to be. By noon it has crossed the Line, as the river does every second, without papers.

7. Original Treatment 2 (television series)

Original treatment (no adaptations). Television treatment: title, logline, genre, premise, short character descriptions of major characters, brief synopsis of the pilot episode.

Title: BORROWED SKY

Logline: In 1975 NASA lends India a satellite for one year, and an ISRO technician rides a motorcycle between forty Orissa villages keeping their first televisions alive, until Emergency officials use the evening viewings to round up men for sterilisation and she starts breaking her own sets.

Genre: Period drama series that darkens into a political thriller; eight episodes of about 50 minutes.

Premise: On 1 August 1975 NASA's ATS-6 satellite, lent to India for one year, starts beaming farming, health and family-planning programmes to televisions in 2,400 villages, each fed by a ten-foot chicken-wire dish. Indira Gandhi's Emergency is five weeks old. In Sambalpur, Orissa, Jharana Meher keeps forty of those sets alive. Each episode a different one goes dead, she rides in on her Rajdoot, and the fault is never in the set. It is a priest who thinks the screen steals his worshippers or a caste quarrel over who sits in front. Two clocks run underneath. The first: the loan ends on 31 July 1976, and her job with it. The second: sterilisation targets rise until district officials notice that the evening viewing puts every man in a village in one place at dusk. By spring the round-ups begin, and Jharana starts breaking her own sets so the men stay home. Saying why could get her jailed. Season one ends the night the sky goes blank.

Major characters:

Jharana Meher (23): weaver's daughter, radio mechanic, and the only woman on the maintenance team; her wage is the first in the house not earned at a loom. She finds a fault in a circuit by smell and one in a village by watching who sits where.

Thomas Varghese (47): Jharana's supervisor, from Kerala, who wants Orissa's uptime to beat Rajasthan's. He hired her over eleven men. By July he must recommend one technician for a permanent post.

Ashok Rath (42): district family-planning officer, a courteous doctor who has delivered enough ninth children to believe in his programme. His targets come from Delhi, and each is larger than the last.

Nilamani Sahu (54): Kendupali's headmaster, who wears the set's key around his neck and oils his hair before every broadcast, in case the screen can see him too. By spring he has a quota of men to "motivate", and one place where they all sit still.

Ed Lindqvist (38): NASA engineer who helped build ATS-6. In his Nebraska hometown the first television sat in the Grange hall. His orders: report on the hardware, stay out of Indian politics.

Gopal Majhi (9): goatherd who skips the morning school broadcast and never misses the evening one. He knows the satellite is a god. He is the youngest of five.

Pilot synopsis: "Facing West"

1 August 1975, the first day of broadcasts. At the maintenance centre Varghese has chalked every village on a blackboard, ready for a tick or a cross. He gives Ed, just in from Maryland, a toolbox and the back seat of Jharana's Rajdoot.

At three o'clock a boy cycles in from Kendupali: the test card is all snow. Twenty-two kilometres of laterite mud later, Ed is red to the knees. Three hundred people sit in the school ground, in rows somebody has clearly argued about. Nilamani, apologising to each row, explains the fault to Ed, because Ed is holding the toolbox. The set is fine. The dish has been turned towards the sunrise and garlanded with marigolds.

Gopal turned it, because gods face east. Ed explains geostationary orbit with a lantern and a guava. Gopal listens politely and keeps both hands on the dish. The evening broadcast is an hour away.

This god, Jharana tells the boy, is like Jagannath riding his chariot to his aunt's house. He is only visiting: he has come from America, he is staying above a lake in Africa, and in a year he goes home. A good host faces his guest. Gopal helps her swing the dish west-south-west, thirty degrees up. The marigolds stay.

Kendupali's first television picture is a test card, and three hundred people applaud it.

At the back stands a man with a notebook, his bush shirt too clean for the road. Jharana takes him for a survey man counting viewers. During the family-planning programme he watches the men, not the screen. Afterwards he introduces himself to Nilamani as Dr Ashok Rath and asks, kindly, how many of these men have three children or more.

Jharana logs the fault: "Dish turned east. Cause: devotion." Gopal leaves the guest a banana. On Varghese's board Kendupali gets its tick; the two villages she did not reach get crosses.

As she leaves, her headlight finds the dish, tilted west towards a borrowed satellite, marigolds on its pole. Beneath it Rath writes "Kendupali: 131 men. All present at dusk."
