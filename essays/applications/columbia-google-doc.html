1. Autobiographical essay

Describe your background, artistic experiences, creative influences, and professional objectives.

I grew up in Gurugram in a family where nobody works in film or photography. There was no one at home to tell me how a set worked, but there was an audience: my own family. In 2016, like many boys my age, I was obsessed with phones and their cameras, Huawei's above all, and I began reworking every photograph from our family trips. My editing went from filters and adjustments to graphic elements, and the first thing I taught myself was object selection: cutting a subject out, blending in a new background and highlighting the focus in an exaggerated, stylish way. Every finished picture went to the printer. I now call those pictures contrast frames, built to stand out before I had asked what they were saying.

Only when I got bored of zooming into pictures on a phone did I pick up a camera. An earlier stint with Filmora, a video editor, had failed, so a camera I could learn felt like a new achievement, and I explored every setting until there was nothing left I had not tried. On trips to Dehradun, where my sibling and two cousins were at school, I shot with a Canon 600D and its kit lens, then traded it in for a nifty fifty on a 7D Mark II, a combination that opened possibilities beyond what a better camera could offer. With it I used manual focus creatively for the first time, played with bokeh and learned how stabilisers work, asking myself why the 7D had no in-body stabilisation when Sony offered it for less. On a family trip to Monaco I taught myself macro, manual HDR blending and long exposures of a changing sky. From above, I caught a Formula One car flat out on the straight, dead centre in the frame, and with an 18-135mm at full zoom, seagulls soaring about fifty metres away. These were balanced frames: symmetrical landscapes, all composition and control.

Composition was not enough for long. I studied under Nitin Rai at the Raghu Rai Center for Photography, the school he founded with his father, the late Raghu Rai. Nitin had spent months teaching me fashion shoots with a fixed camera. Then, on one assignment, he called in a model for a commercial shoot, and I hid in a corner trying to get better pictures than his Vogue-style studio set-up would give. I got some beautiful frames, and so did he. That day he saw what I really loved: media creation and a free-moving camera. In my last month there I invited a friend to model for me; she was a complete amateur, and I covered three outfits in two hours. I left probably way too confident, with a lens that no longer wanted to stay still.

The other source of that confidence was The Classroom, a five-part web series that friends and I made at Suncity School and released on YouTube, my first real video work. I co-directed, shot and edited it, and I also organised it, finding places to shoot, scheduling shoots around exams and keeping classmates involved. None of us had a name for that last part of the work.

Some of that confidence went into approaching a musician at school whose music had blown my mind in 11th grade. He was making things for fun then, as I was, and he is steadily releasing and producing now. We shot many, many pictures on a secluded hilly road outside the city, and he loved them. The very next day, at his request, we shot a sequence made of stills in which he stays steady while the background moves. He loved that so much he would not risk a new location, so we went back once more, and this time it was all video: an intro of him driving in, opening the back of the car, and a graphic title at the top. On that road my stills began to move.

The stills did not stop when I started shooting video. The day before I left for Europe, I did a professional shoot for SHLAY, a senior's new boutique clothing brand. By then I carried a Nikon Z6 with a heavy 24-70 and had been convinced the camera was doing the work, until I handed it to someone else and saw that knowledge made the difference. On that shoot I felt the urge for a meaningful frame: not the contrast frame of my editing days, not the balanced frame of my landscapes, but an impact frame.

In France I hit a point where I could not stop using the camera, not because of the architecture and not because of the camera. I shot on autopilot: motion long exposures, frozen water, birds, strangers, and my own family as if on black-and-white film. I had built my colour profiles before travelling, and I never felt the need to edit a frame. That trip settled for me the difference between a career and the thing I would do anyway. My family never knew it would become a career; my parents did not talk to us about work until my senior years at school. Today their houses are full of my polished prints, and although I sometimes miss the amateur auto-exposure moments when everyone was made to smile, I prefer candids now, because now the images bring smiles.

At Whistling Woods International in Mumbai, where I trained as a cinematographer, my camera turned round. Student sets kept asking me to be their behind-the-scenes photographer, for stills and video, more of them than I could cover, so I committed to shoots in advance, and even on days with no set of my own I wanted to be on one, for the beautiful sets, the people at work and the candid moments. I was photographing the people making films rather than the films, and from behind the scenes I could watch every department at work, not only the camera team. That was my bridge to producing.

The sets on my CV, most recently as a camera assistant on a project under producer Boney Kapoor, have shown me the other side of that view from inside one department: on set after set, problems reach the camera that started earlier, in decisions about the schedule, the budget, locations or cast, and my department can only work around them. I want to be where those decisions are made. Commerce at Suncity School had already taught me the questions asked there: what something costs, who pays for it and who it is for. The word none of us had for the organising side of The Classroom is producing: deciding which stories get made and setting them up to be made well. I have not yet worked in a production office, developed a script with a writer over many drafts, or handled the budgets, schedules, financing and distribution that decide whether a film gets made and seen. That is what I am applying to Creative Producing to learn.

My creative influences have been people at close range: a teacher who noticed what I loved though he was teaching something else, a musician whose requests set my pictures moving, a family who were my first audience, and the crews who let me photograph them at work.

The three pieces in this application are the stories I want to produce. In ROLL CALL, my writing sample, a school drill master in 1951 Rajasthan must enrol twenty women under their own names on India's first electoral roll, though they will only speak from behind a purdah screen. In ONLY ONCE, my film prompt scene, a young wife stuck near the top of a ferris wheel at the Gangasagar Mela faces the mother-in-law she and her husband came to leave behind. In my feature treatment, a boy in Mizoram in 2007, as the bamboo flowers and the rats swarm, finds his grandfather hoarding rice. Each is about who a family or a country counts and who it leaves out of the frame, and I want to make them with humour and without pity.

Columbia's first year would put me inside the process I used to photograph from behind the scenes: producers write and direct short films alongside the writers and directors, and produce a classmate's film, so I would learn how a script is built before I learn how it is paid for. Later come feature film development and financing, the business of film, and a thesis film I would produce. Columbia graduate Apoorva Charan, an Indian-born producer whose film Joyland won a prize at Cannes, has the kind of career I want.

I began with contrast frames; then I learned balance, wanted meaning, let the camera move and turned it towards the people making the film. Producing is the next turn: deciding, long before anyone rolls, which stories deserve a frame at all. After a few years in development and production, I plan to build a studio in India of 8 to 30 people that develops and produces its own slate of films and series for audiences in India and abroad. The stories in this application ask who gets counted. If the studio can hire for talent rather than connections, and give newcomers paid roles, real responsibility and the explanations I had to find for myself, it will be counting people in on both sides of the camera.

4. Feature film treatment

A complete dramatic story with major characters, plot developments, and clear resolution. Must differ from the writing sample.

Genre: Folk-horror drama

Log line: Mizoram, 2007: as rats swarm the flowering bamboo, a boy selling their tails for a guitar finds his grandfather hoarding rice, as the family did in the 1959 famine.

Around a Mizoram hill village the bamboo flowers for the first time since 1959. The old people say mautam comes to collect a debt. Rats breed on the fruit. Mapuia, 16, needs four thousand rupees for a red guitar, at two rupees a tail from the state. Sangi, 15, next door, brings him tails from other villages at a rupee. He cashes them at two, buys them back from the boy paid to burn them, and cashes them again. Every week the rats are bigger.

Pu Thanga, his grandfather, never speaks of 1959. Since the flowering he has built two water tanks that hold no water.

In November the bamboo dies standing. Collecting by torchlight, Mapuia finds rats three deep on the tank lids. By morning every field and granary is stripped. The tanks are full of rice.

In 1959, Thanga admits, his father sold hoarded rice at ten times the price while the village ate roots. It kept this family alive. He will sell the same way.

By Christmas Sangi's family is eating banana stems. Mapuia cuts the padlocks and gives the rice to the Young Mizo Association to share: tlawmngaihna, putting others first. He hands Sangi his guitar money: the second rupee on every tail she sold him. Thanga will not eat from the tanks. The last image: a week later, Pu Thanga, hungry for the first time in his life, queuing behind Sangi for his own rice.
