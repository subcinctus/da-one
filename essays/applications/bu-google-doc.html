1. Personal statement

Why do you believe the Master's in Media Ventures is the right graduate program for you? What are your goals for the future and what do you hope to accomplish with this degree?

I came to film from a commerce classroom and a family where nobody works in film or photography, and the first pictures I cared about were our own. Before I owned a camera, I edited every photograph from our family trips on my phone, exaggerating whatever I wanted the eye to land on, and had them all printed. Those were contrast frames, made to stand out. Trips with a camera taught me the balanced frame. It was not until I hid in a corner at the Raghu Rai Center for Photography, during a commercial shoot with a model, looking for better frames than the studio set-up offered, that I saw the pictures I wanted were impact frames, about people. I now plan to build a media company in India around that kind of work, and I am applying to the Master's in Media Ventures to learn to run it as a business.

At Suncity School I studied economics, business studies and accountancy, so I can read a budget. What those subjects did not teach is how media is sold: audiences in India now watch on phones, streaming services and social platforms, and a company must choose where each project goes and how it will earn back its cost.

The frame started to move with The Classroom, a five-part web series my friends and I produced ourselves at school and released on YouTube. Besides co-directing, shooting and editing it, I organised it, finding places to shoot, scheduling shoots around exams and keeping classmates involved.

My first media venture, though I did not call it that, was Mitti to Matka, which I joined during COVID, still at school. Gurugram's potters had lost their markets, so we bought and auctioned their pots at fair prices, worked with them on designs, and supplied food and sanitary supplies to their families. As media manager I made the photographs, videos and graphics, ran the website and handled the administration. Buyers saw the pots through my photographs, so a picture could decide what a family earned that week: the plainest impact frame I know. The money was meant for those families, and that made me careful with it.

I trained as a cinematographer at Whistling Woods International in Mumbai, completing a B.Sc. in Filmmaking, Cinematography and Direction, awarded by the Tata Institute of Social Sciences in 2025. Student sets kept asking me to shoot their behind-the-scenes stills and video, more than I could cover, which let me watch every department at work. I was a camera assistant on Khauf at Matchbox Studios and have been one since October 2025 at Bayview Projects under producer Boney Kapoor. There I saw how earlier decisions about schedule, budget, locations and cast shaped what reached the camera. Two jobs took me closer to those decisions. On the direction team of a Finolex commercial for Early Man Productions I prepared the production sheets, cast references and pre-production meeting deck, and ran costume trials, and for Growth Jockey I planned a Sleepyhead Mattresses shoot, from the equipment and script to the offer and audience the film was aimed at.

The programme is built in the order I need. In Boston I would learn how money moves through media and how to analyse a creative venture's market, and in Los Angeles I would intern in the industry, develop my own venture as a thesis and pitch it to executives and investors, the closest thing to building the first version of my studio while I still have teachers around me. I have not yet raised money for a film or series, built a business model for one or worked in a production office. By the end of the programme I want to raise the money and build the model myself, judge an opportunity and read an audience through data.

After the degree I plan to spend a few years in development and production, then build a creative studio in India of 8 to 30 people with a slate of films, series and digital content, run on clear budgets and fair contracts, releasing some work directly to audiences online. Nobody in my family works in film, and I know how much one early chance can matter, so the studio would hire talented people who start out the same way, train them, pay them fairly and give them responsibility early. If it can give a handful of them their first real chance, that would be an impact frame wider than any I could compose alone.

2. Case study

A company, brand, or individual making an impact by bringing together content and technology in important ways. What is it doing, how is it using technology and content to lead and serve its audience, how is it innovating, and how does it gain competitive advantage (or, for a non-profit, create community impact)?

Pocket FM: building a content business for the phone

I was trained to think in frames, so it says something that the company I chose for this case study has no picture at all. In India the phone is the screen that matters most, yet many people use it while they are doing something else, commuting, cooking or working, at moments when they cannot look at a screen. Pocket FM, founded in Bengaluru in 2018, built a business around that habit. It produces and distributes long-running audio series, and it has grown from India into other markets, including the United States.

What the company does. Pocket FM publishes stories in short episodes released every day, in several Indian languages and in English, and listeners can start for free before paying to unlock episodes or subscribing. The genres are ones mass audiences already like, such as romance, thrillers, fantasy and family drama, so what sets the company apart is the format, which is built for the phone and for daily listening.

How it uses technology. The company treats content as a product to be tested and improved. It studies how listeners behave, which episodes keep them listening, where they drop off and what makes them pay, and that data shapes what gets commissioned and how stories are paced. It has also built up a large pool of writers and voice artists, with tools and processes that let it produce at a scale traditional studios cannot match.

How it uses content. The stories are designed for serial listening. Each episode ends on a hook, characters run for hundreds of episodes, and a successful series can extend into new seasons or formats. Instead of betting on a few expensive titles, the company makes many affordable ones and puts more money into the ones audiences keep coming back to, so its slate is shaped by what listeners actually do.

How it innovates and gains an advantage. Pocket FM's position is hard to copy because it combines three things: a format matched to Indian listening habits, a monetisation model built on small, frequent payments, and a feedback loop between data and creative decisions. Large streaming platforms compete for attention with expensive video, while Pocket FM competes for the minutes when people cannot watch a screen at all. That lets it reach audiences outside the big cities and outside English, which many media companies have struggled to serve.

Questions and risks. The model raises real questions. Data-led commissioning can favour formula over originality, and fast growth can put pressure on how writers and voice artists are paid and credited. Because Pocket FM needs a steady flow of new episodes, it depends on those writers and voice artists continuing to work with it, which makes how it treats them a business question as much as an ethical one.

What it means for me. Pocket FM grew by finding a format and audiences that established media companies had overlooked, and that is the kind of opening I would look for. The studio I plan to build in India, of 8 to 30 people, would be far smaller, but some of the same methods would still apply: start from how the audience actually watches or listens, test ideas cheaply, and put more money behind what the audience responds to. As a cinematographer I learned to decide what goes into a frame, and Pocket FM is a reminder that a media business first has to find out where its audience's attention really is, and only then decide what to put in front of it. Audience data would feed into creative decisions, but I would still make those decisions with the writers. In a studio that size the writers and performers are most of what the company has, so I would settle their pay and credit in writing before work starts.
