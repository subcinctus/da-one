1. Personal statement

Why do you believe the Master's in Media Ventures is the right graduate program for you? What are your goals for the future and what do you hope to accomplish with this degree?

The first thing I taught myself on a phone, in 2016, was object selection: cutting a subject free, setting it against a new background and highlighting what mattered in an exaggerated, stylish way. Like any boy my age I was obsessed with phones, and I reworked every photograph from our family trips, then had them all printed. They were contrast frames, made to grab the eye, and my family was their first audience, though nobody at home imagined they would lead to a career.

Bored with zooming into phone pictures, I picked up a camera. On trips to Dehradun I shot with a Canon 600D, then traded it for a 7D Mark II with a nifty fifty, a swap that opened more possibilities than a better camera could have. It raised what I now see as a business question: why did the 7D lack the in-body stabilisation Sony offered for less? In Monaco I taught myself long exposures of a changing sky, followed soaring seagulls about fifty metres away at full zoom, and from above framed an F1 car dead centre, flat out on the straight: balanced frames, all composition and control.

My pictures turned to people at the Raghu Rai Center for Photography, where I studied under Nitin Rai. When he brought in a model for a commercial shoot, I hid in a corner, chasing better pictures than the Vogue-style studio set-up, and came away with some beautiful frames; so did he. Nitin could see that media creation and a free-moving camera meant far more to me than the fixed-camera fashion work he had spent months teaching. In my last month I invited a friend, a complete amateur, to model, and we covered three outfits in two hours. I left probably way too confident.

The frame was already moving at school, where friends and I made The Classroom, a five-part web series for YouTube and my first real video work. Besides co-directing, shooting and editing it, I organised it, scouting places to shoot, fitting shoot days around exams and keeping classmates involved.

By then I shot on a Nikon Z6 with a heavy 24-70 and thought the camera did the work, until I handed it to someone else and saw that knowledge made the difference. Shooting for SHLAY, a senior's new boutique brand, the day before I left for Europe, I felt the urge for an impact frame, neither a contrast frame nor a balanced one. In France I shot on autopilot, birds, strangers and my family as if on black-and-white film, through colour profiles built before leaving, and never edited a frame. France settled the difference between a passion and a career.

Whistling Woods International trained me as a cinematographer, and there the free-moving camera Nitin had noticed turned round to face the crew. Student sets wanted me behind the scenes for stills and video, more sets than I could cover, so I committed to shoots in advance; even on free days I wanted to be on one, for the people at work and the candid moments. I was photographing the people making films, not the films, and watching the whole set at work. My CV lists the sets I have worked on; as a camera assistant I have watched problems reach the camera that began earlier, in a schedule, a budget or a casting choice, and I want to be where those decisions are made.

Media Ventures would teach me the business behind them. I have not yet raised money for a film, built a business model or worked in a production office. In Boston I would learn how money moves through media and how to analyse a creative venture's market; in Los Angeles I would intern, develop my own venture as a thesis and pitch it to executives and investors, a first version of my studio built with teachers around me.

After the degree I plan to spend a few years in development and production, then build a creative studio in India of 8 to 30 people making films, series and digital content, some released straight to audiences online, as The Classroom was. Nobody in my family works in film; my chances came from a senior who trusted me with SHLAY and student sets that asked for me, so the studio would hire for talent and trust newcomers early. If it gives a handful of them their first real chance, that would be an impact frame wider than any I could compose alone.

2. Case study

A company, brand, or individual making an impact by bringing together content and technology in important ways. What is it doing, how is it using technology and content to lead and serve its audience, how is it innovating, and how does it gain competitive advantage (or, for a non-profit, create community impact)?

Pocket FM: building a content business for the phone

I was trained to think in frames, so it says something that the company I chose for this case study has no picture at all. In India the phone is the screen that matters most, yet many people use it while they are doing something else, commuting, cooking or working, at moments when they cannot look at a screen. Pocket FM, founded in Bengaluru in 2018, built a business around that habit. It produces and distributes long-running audio series, and it has grown from India into other markets, including the United States.

What the company does. Pocket FM publishes stories in short episodes released every day, in several Indian languages and in English, and listeners can start for free before paying to unlock episodes or subscribing. The genres are ones mass audiences already like, such as romance, thrillers, fantasy and family drama, so what sets the company apart is the format, which is built for the phone and for daily listening.

How it uses technology. The company treats content as a product to be tested and improved. It studies how listeners behave, which episodes keep them listening, where they drop off and what makes them pay, and that data shapes what gets commissioned and how stories are paced. It has also built up a large pool of writers and voice artists, with tools and processes that let it produce at a scale traditional studios cannot match.

How it uses content. The stories are designed for serial listening. Each episode ends on a hook, characters run for hundreds of episodes, and a successful series can extend into new seasons or formats. Instead of betting on a few expensive titles, the company makes many affordable ones and puts more money into the ones audiences keep coming back to, so its slate is shaped by what listeners actually do.

How it innovates and gains an advantage. Pocket FM's position is hard to copy because it combines three things: a format matched to Indian listening habits, a monetisation model built on small, frequent payments, and a feedback loop between data and creative decisions. Large streaming platforms compete for attention with expensive video, while Pocket FM competes for the minutes when people cannot watch a screen at all. That lets it reach audiences outside the big cities and outside English, which many media companies have struggled to serve.

Questions and risks. The model raises real questions. Data-led commissioning can favour formula over originality, and fast growth can put pressure on how writers and voice artists are paid and credited. Because Pocket FM needs a steady flow of new episodes, it depends on those writers and voice artists continuing to work with it, which makes how it treats them a business question as much as an ethical one.

What it means for me. Pocket FM grew by finding a format and audiences that established media companies had overlooked, and that is the kind of opening I would look for. I first saw the value of building on what people respond to in 11th grade, with a musician at school whose songs had blown me away. He loved the pictures from our first shoot on a secluded hilly road, so the next day, at his request, we shot a sequence of stills in which he stays steady while the background moves, which today would be done with Gaussian splats. He loved the sequence so much that he would not risk a new location, so we went back a third time and shot all video: him driving in, opening the back of the car, a graphic title at the top. The studio I plan to build in India would be much smaller than Pocket FM, but some of the same methods would apply: start from how the audience actually watches or listens, test ideas cheaply, and put more money behind what the audience responds to. As a cinematographer I learned to decide what goes into a frame, and Pocket FM is a reminder that a media business first has to find out where its audience's attention really is, and only then decide what to put in front of it. Audience data would feed into creative decisions, but I would still make those decisions with the writers. In a studio that small the writers and performers are most of what the company has, so I would settle their pay and credit in writing before work starts.
