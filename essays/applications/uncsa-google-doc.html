Karan Chaudhary

Letter of Intent, MFA in Filmmaking (Creative Producing), Fall 2027

On most sets I have worked on as a cinematographer, the problems that reached the camera had started much earlier, in a schedule, a budget or a location, often with a cost no one was in charge of. By then, all the camera team could do was adjust. The decisions that shape a frame are made before anyone rolls, and I want to be responsible for them. That is why I am applying to the Creative Producing concentration at UNCSA.

Where I come from

Nobody in my family works in film or photography, and although my parents never quite expected this to become my career, my eye had begun on a phone, editing family photographs into what I now call contrast frames, pictures made to stand out, before trips with a camera taught me the balanced frame of landscapes. While I studied commerce, accountancy included, at Suncity School, I learned studio fashion photography under Nitin Rai. At the time the accountancy and the photography felt unrelated. As a producer I will need both.

At the Raghu Rai Center for Photography, during a commercial shoot with a model, I hid in a corner looking for frames better than the studio set-up allowed, and came away with some good ones, as did the photographer leading the shoot. I was happier with a camera free to move than with a fixed studio set-up, and wanted frames with impact, about people rather than the set.

The camera started to move when a group of us at school made The Classroom, a five-part web series we released on YouTube. I co-directed, shot and edited it, and without realising it I also produced it: I found places to shoot, scheduled shoots around exams and kept my classmates involved. When an actor dropped out at the last second on one shoot day, we made up a new character on the spot, a funny sports teacher, and I played him. Our scriptwriter was at home, so we rewrote the lines together remotely; I got hold of a standard tracksuit for the costume without spending anything, and the episode's story still held together.

During COVID I was media manager for Mitti to Matka, an initiative supporting Gurugram's potters, which I describe on the second page of my resume. Their families depended on us, and I learned to tell people honestly what we could and could not deliver, as I mean to with writers, crews and investors.

What my work has taught me

I completed a B.Sc. in Filmmaking, Cinematography and Direction, a degree awarded by the Tata Institute of Social Sciences, at Whistling Woods International in Mumbai in 2025, and while I studied I worked as an assistant cinematographer on five larger-scale short films, as lead cinematographer on independent films, and on DIT and colour. Student sets also kept asking me to shoot their behind-the-scenes stills and video, more than I could cover, so I committed to shoots in advance; with the camera turned round, I watched every department at work. I was a camera assistant on Khauf at Matchbox Studios under Pankaj Kumar, and since October 2025 I have held the same role at Bayview Projects, on a project under producer Boney Kapoor. On a Finolex commercial for Early Man Productions I was on the direction team, preparing the production sheets, cast references and PPM deck and running costume trials, and for Growth Jockey I planned the Sleepyhead Mattresses shoot: the equipment, the script, and the offer and audience the film was aimed at.

I have not yet worked in a production office, developed material with a writer, put financing together, drawn up a film's budget, or taken a film to distributors. That is what I am applying to learn.

My creative interests

I want to produce grounded drama with humour, specific to India and made on budgets where the money ends up on screen, about ordinary people who run into something far bigger than they are, and find their own way through it. I have written two treatments of this kind: a comic drama about a young man in Ladakh who must help his grandmother bring back their village's stream with ice from across the Line of Control, and a period series about a young technician keeping Orissa's first village televisions running in 1975. In both, the frame stays on the person, however large the world around them.

My goals and why UNCSA

After the MFA I plan to work in development and production for a few years, then build a creative studio in India of 8 to 30 people that develops its own slate of films and series and gives talented people with no contacts in film a way in. To run it I need to understand development, financing, intellectual property, marketing and distribution well enough to make decisions.

In my first year I would share classes with the MFA screenwriters, which is how I want to learn development, alongside writers whose projects I may one day produce, offering them a camera department's sense of what each scene will take to shoot. Two courses, The Development Process and The Business of Producing, cover the foundations I lack. The thesis, a three-project slate presented to industry leaders with financing, production, marketing and distribution plans, is close to what the studio's first slate will need. The weekly faculty mentor meetings matter too: I have learned most from people willing to explain the work.

So far I have decided what belongs in a frame from wherever I stood: a corner of a studio, behind the camera, or on a student set with my lens on the crew. I want to leave UNCSA able to make those decisions earlier, and ready to start a studio where people who begin as I did, with no contacts in film, can make them too.

2. Resume, page 2 essay

The second page of the resume should consist of an essay in which you describe how you have integrated your extra-curricular activities and/or community service into our world artistically as well as personally.

Almost everything I did outside class involved a camera, and each activity pointed it at something different.

From 2016 I edited every photograph from our family trips on my phone, moving from filters to cutouts and new backgrounds, and had each one printed. I now call them contrast frames, images built to stand out, whatever they said about the people in them. My family's homes are still full of my pictures, though these days I prefer the candid ones, because now the images bring smiles.

Outside school I studied studio fashion photography under Nitin Rai at the Raghu Rai Center for Photography, which taught me to look closely at light and at faces, and as the International Press Photographer for Suncity MUN I learned to work quickly in crowded rooms and to keep asking what the viewer needed to see.

With friends I made The Classroom, a five-part web series we produced ourselves and put on YouTube. Besides co-directing, shooting and editing it, I looked for locations and planned our shoot days around the exam timetable. It was the first time I organised the people, places and time a film needs, and the first time my frames had to move.

During COVID I was media manager for Mitti to Matka, a Gurugram initiative supporting local potters whose sales had collapsed. We bought and auctioned their pots at fair prices, helped improve designs, and delivered food and sanitary supplies to their families. I made the graphics, stills and videos that showed buyers who these potters were, ran the website and handled the administration.

Artistically, Mitti to Matka gave my images a job to do. Buyers judged the pots by my photographs, so a picture could decide what a family earned that week, and the question I had skipped with the family prints, what a photograph says about the people in it, became the one that mattered.

Personally, it made me careful with money. The funds we handled were meant for the potters' families, so anything we spent badly was money a family did not receive. I felt responsible for those families. We could sell pots and deliver supplies, but a few young volunteers could not undo what the lockdown had done to that community.

I want to bring both into producing: care with money that belongs to other people, and attention to how people are shown on screen. I began by making frames that stood out. What I produce, and the studio I hope to build in India, should be judged by whether their frames are fair to the people inside them.
