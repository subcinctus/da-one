Letter of Intent, MFA in Filmmaking, Creative Producing concentration, Fall 2027

I learned to make pictures before I learned to take them. In 2016, like many boys my age, I was obsessed with phones and their cameras, Huawei's above all, and I became the one in my family who edited every photograph from our trips and had each one printed. I moved from filters and adjustments to graphic elements, and the first thing I taught myself was object selection: cutting out a subject, blending in a new background and highlighting the focus of the image in an exaggerated, stylish way. I now call those contrast frames. They were built to stand out, and standing out was all I asked of them.

Only when I got bored of zooming into pictures on a phone did I pick up a camera. After a failed stint with Filmora, a video editor, the camera felt like a new achievement, and I worked through it until there was nothing on it I had not tried. On a family trip to Monaco I taught myself macro photography, manual HDR blending and long exposures of a changing sky. From above, I caught an F1 car flat out on the straight, dead centre in the frame, and, at full zoom, seagulls soaring about fifty metres away. These were balanced frames, symmetrical and controlled.

The people came next. I spent about eight months at the Raghu Rai Center for Photography, founded by the photographer Raghu Rai, studying under his son, Nitin Rai. On one assignment Nitin called in a model for a commercial shoot, and I hid in a corner trying to get better pictures than the Vogue-style studio set-up. I got some beautiful frames, and so did he. What I had wanted in that corner, I now call an impact frame: a picture that means something because of the person in it. He saw that I loved media creation and a free-moving camera far more than the fixed-camera fashion shoots he had spent months teaching me. In my last month I invited a friend, a complete amateur, to model for me, and I covered three outfits in two hours. I left, I admit, probably way too confident.

In that mood I approached a musician at my school whose music had blown my mind in eleventh grade. Like me, he was making things for fun and hoping for a career in it, and today he is still steadily releasing and producing music. We shot many, many pictures on a secluded hilly road outside the city, and he loved them. The very next day, at his request, we shot a sequence of stills in which he stays steady while the background moves, something now done with Gaussian splats. He loved that so much he would not risk a new location, so we went back once more, this time all video: an intro of him driving in, opening the back of the car, and a graphic title at the top. That was where my stills began to move.

At Whistling Woods International, where I trained as a cinematographer, the camera turned round. Student sets kept asking me to shoot their behind-the-scenes stills and video, more than I could cover, so I committed to shoots in advance, and even on days without a set of my own I wanted to be on one, for beautiful sets, people at work and candid moments. The free-moving camera Nitin had noticed now had its subject. While the film's camera watched the scene, mine watched the people making it, in every department, and I wanted to know what had shaped their day.

Now that I work as a camera assistant, I see that the problems reaching the camera started much earlier, in a schedule, a budget, a location or a casting choice. By then, all the camera team can do is adjust. I want to be where those decisions are made. I have not yet worked in a production office, developed material with a writer, put financing together, drawn up a film's budget or taken a film to distributors, and that is what I am applying to UNCSA to learn.

I want to produce grounded drama with humour, specific to India and made on budgets where the money ends up on screen, about ordinary people who run into something far bigger than they are. I have written two treatments of this kind: a comic drama about a young man in Ladakh who must help his grandmother bring back their village's stream with ice from across the Line of Control, and a period series about a young technician keeping Orissa's first village televisions running in 1975. In both, the frame stays on the person, however large the world around them.

At UNCSA I would learn development in first-year classes shared with the MFA screenwriters whose projects I may one day produce, bringing a camera department's sense of what each scene will take to shoot. The Development Process and The Business of Producing cover the foundations I lack. The weekly faculty mentor meetings matter too: at the Raghu Rai Center I saw how much a teacher who watches you work can see.

After the MFA I plan to work in development and production for a few years, then build a studio in India of 8 to 30 people, one that develops films and series of its own and gives talented people with no contacts in film a way in. The UNCSA thesis, a three-project slate presented to industry leaders with financing, production, marketing and distribution plans, is close to what that studio's first slate will need. I learned to make pictures before I learned to take them, and producing, as I now understand it, is deciding what reaches the frame long before anyone takes a picture. That is what I want to learn at UNCSA, and the ground I want that studio built on.

2. Resume, page 2 essay

The second page of the resume should consist of an essay in which you describe how you have integrated your extra-curricular activities and/or community service into our world artistically as well as personally.

Outside class, my camera kept leading me towards other people: first the friends who wanted to make something with me, then the families who needed my pictures to work for them.

With friends at Suncity School I made The Classroom, a five-part web series for YouTube, and it was my first real video work. Until then I had only ever made single pictures. Now a frame had to hand the story on to the next one, and most of the work happened before the camera came out: finding places to shoot, building a timetable around exams, and keeping friends who had their own lives coming back to set. Artistically, it taught me that a frame is one part of something longer. Personally, it was the first time other people's time depended on my planning, and when it was done I found confidence in what I could make.

During COVID I was media manager for Mitti to Matka, a Gurugram initiative supporting local potters whose sales had collapsed. We bought and auctioned their pots at fair prices, helped improve designs, and delivered food and sanitary supplies to their families.

Artistically, it gave my images a job to do. Buyers judged the pots by the photographs and videos I made, so a picture could decide what a family earned that week. I learned to ask whether a photograph was fair to the potter behind the pot, and not only whether it would sell.

Personally, doing the administration made me careful with money. The funds were meant for the potters' families, so anything we spent badly was money a family did not receive. I felt responsible for those families. We could sell pots and deliver supplies, but a few young volunteers could not undo what the lockdown had done to that community.

I want to bring all of this into producing: the organising I first did for friends, care with money that belongs to other people, and attention to how people are shown on screen. What I produce should be judged by whether its frames are fair to the people inside them.

4. Video essay

What do you believe is unique about you that contributes to your storytelling ability?

Talking points:

1. Opening: "I'm Karan Chaudhary, from Gurugram. These days I'm a camera assistant, and I've had a camera in my hands since school."

2. My answer in one line: "I've told stories with whatever I had, and I've learned that what I know matters more than what I'm holding." On trips to Dehradun, where my sibling and two cousins were at school, I shot on an entry-level Canon with its kit lens, then traded it for a cheap fifty-millimetre lens on a camera built for sport and wildlife. "That combination opened possibilities beyond what a better camera could offer." That's where I first used manual focus creatively and played with bokeh. Later, when I shot for SHLAY, a senior's new boutique clothing brand, I had a better camera and a heavy professional lens and was convinced the camera was doing the work, until I handed it to someone else. The difference was knowledge.

3. I ask what things cost. Even as a teenager, I wanted to know why my camera had no in-body stabilisation when Sony offered it for less. Then I studied commerce at school, accountancy included. "I can read a balance sheet and a lighting plan." So I look for the version of a story that can actually be made well.

4. I decide the look before the day. Before a trip to France I built my own colour profiles, and I never felt the need to edit a frame. I couldn't stop using the camera, "not because of the architecture, not because of the camera." I shot on autopilot: motion long exposures, frozen water, birds, strangers, and my own family as if on black-and-white film. What made the difference was the preparation.

5. I wait for the real moment. My family never expected this to become my career, and my parents didn't talk to us about work until my senior years at school. Now their houses are full of my polished prints. I sometimes miss the amateur auto-exposure moments when everyone was made to smile, but I prefer candids now, "because now the images bring smiles."

6. Close: "Knowledge over gear, an eye on cost, the look decided before the day, and patience for the real moment. Those are a photographer's habits, and a producer's too. That's what I'd bring to UNCSA, and to every story I produce."
